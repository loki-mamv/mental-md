What Is a Mental Health Screening, and What Happens After One?

The short answer: A mental health screening is a short questionnaire, usually 2 to 9 questions, that flags people who may have depression or anxiety. It is not a diagnosis. A high score means you need a fuller evaluation with a clinician. In the U.S., the USPSTF recommends depression screening for all adults, and most health plans cover it at no cost.

How is a screening different from a diagnosis?

A screening sorts people. A diagnosis names a condition. The two use different tools and take different amounts of time.

A screening is quick. You fill out a form in the waiting room or on a tablet, and it takes a few minutes. It asks how often you have had certain symptoms over the past 2 weeks. The score tells your clinician whether to look closer.

A diagnosis takes a conversation. A clinician asks about your history, how long symptoms have lasted, how they affect your life, and what else could explain them. Thyroid problems, medications, grief, and substance use can all look like depression on a form.

The USPSTF is direct about this. Its anxiety recommendation states that screening tools alone are not enough to diagnose an anxiety disorder. A positive screen needs a confirmatory diagnostic assessment.

What tools are used, and what do the scores mean?

Most primary care offices use a small set of free, validated questionnaires. Each question is scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day).

  • PHQ-2. Two questions about low mood and loss of interest over the past 2 weeks. Scores run from 0 to 6. In the original validation study of 6,000 patients, a score of 3 or higher was the best cutoff. It caught 83% of people with major depression.
  • PHQ-9. Nine questions, one for each core symptom of depression, including one about thoughts of self-harm. Scores run from 0 to 27. In the 2001 validation study, scores of 5, 10, 15, and 20 marked mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe depression. A score of 10 or higher had 88% sensitivity and 88% specificity for major depression.
  • GAD-7. Seven questions about worry, restlessness, and tension. Scores run from 0 to 21. The 2006 validation study in 15 primary care clinics found it was a valid screen for generalized anxiety disorder. In the USPSTF evidence review, a cutoff of 10 or higher caught 79% of people with generalized anxiety disorder.

Offices often start with the two-question version. If that score is high, you then get the longer form. Other tools exist for specific groups, such as the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale for new parents and the Geriatric Depression Scale for older adults.

A score is a signal, not a verdict. Even at the standard PHQ-9 cutoff, the USPSTF review found the tool correctly identified 85% of people with major depression and 85% of those without it. That leaves room for both false alarms and missed cases.

Who should be screened, and how often?

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) sets the national screening recommendations. Here is where they stand:

  • Depression in adults: recommended for all adults, including pregnant and postpartum people and adults 65 and older (grade B, June 2023).
  • Anxiety in adults: recommended for adults 64 and younger, including pregnant and postpartum people (grade B, June 2023). For adults 65 and older, the evidence was insufficient to recommend for or against.
  • Depression in young people: recommended for adolescents aged 12 to 18. The evidence was insufficient for children 11 and younger (October 2022).
  • Anxiety in young people: recommended for children and adolescents aged 8 to 18. The evidence was insufficient for children 7 and younger (October 2022).

The USPSTF does not set a fixed interval. It suggests screening anyone not screened before, then using judgment. Risk factors, other health conditions, and life events such as a new baby or a loss can prompt another screen.

These recommendations apply to people without symptoms. If you already feel unwell, you do not need to wait for a screening. Our guide on when to seek therapy covers the signs that it is time to reach out.

Who pays for a mental health screening?

Under the Affordable Care Act, most health plans must cover certain preventive services with no copay or coinsurance. This applies even if you have not met your deductible.

HealthCare.gov lists depression screening for adults as one of these services. It also lists depression screening for adolescents beginning routinely at age 12, plus behavioral assessments for children.

There are two catches. The service is generally free only from an in-network provider, and HealthCare.gov notes that $0 cost is not guaranteed in all cases. Also, the free part is the screening. The follow-up evaluation and any treatment are billed under your normal mental health benefits.

If you are uninsured, community health centers and many primary care offices still screen routinely. Our breakdown of what therapy costs without insurance covers lower-cost options for the next step.

What happens after a positive screen?

A positive screen starts a process. The USPSTF says the benefit of screening depends on what comes next: people who screen positive need further evaluation and, if appropriate, evidence-based care or a referral. Here is what that usually looks like:

  1. A follow-up conversation. Your clinician asks about your symptoms in more detail, often the same day. They may give you the longer questionnaire if you started with a short one.
  2. Ruling out other causes. Depending on your history, this can include a physical exam, a medication review, or blood work.
  3. A diagnosis, or not. Many people with a high score do not meet criteria for a disorder. Stress, poor sleep, or a hard stretch of life can raise a score for a while.
  4. A treatment plan or referral. Options include therapy, medication, or both. Some primary care clinics treat depression and anxiety directly. Others refer you to a therapist, psychologist, or psychiatrist. Our guide to psychiatrists, psychologists, and therapists explains who does what.
  5. Follow-up. Clinicians often repeat the same questionnaire at later visits. A falling score is one way to see whether treatment is working.

The USPSTF warns that screening without adequate follow-up can lead to treatment failures or harm. If you screen positive and nobody calls you back, ask for the next step. For condition-specific guides, depression.md covers depression in more depth.

What if the screen flags suicide risk?

The last question on the PHQ-9 asks about thoughts of death or of hurting yourself. Any answer other than "not at all" should prompt a follow-up question from your clinician. It does not mean you will be hospitalized.

A clinician will ask follow-up questions. These cover how often the thoughts come, whether you have a plan, and what keeps you safe. Most of the time, the result is a safety plan and a faster link to care. Emergency care is for people at immediate risk.

The USPSTF found the evidence insufficient to recommend routine suicide risk screening for adults, children, or adolescents who have no symptoms. That is a statement about research gaps, not about safety. If a depression screen raises the concern, clinicians still act on it.

If you are in crisis at any time, call or text 988, or chat online. SAMHSA describes 988 as 24/7, judgment-free support for mental health and substance use concerns. Veterans can dial 988 and press 1, and Spanish speakers can dial 988 and press 2.

The bottom line

A mental health screening is a quick questionnaire that tells your clinician whether to look closer. It is not a label. The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are the most common tools, and a score of 10 or higher on either usually prompts a fuller evaluation. The USPSTF recommends depression screening for adults and teens, and anxiety screening for adults under 65 and children 8 and older. Most plans cover the screening at no cost. What matters most is the follow-up: an evaluation, a plan, and someone checking in.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I take a PHQ-9 or GAD-7 on my own at home?

Yes. Both questionnaires are free and widely available. A home score can help you decide whether to book a visit, but it cannot diagnose anything. Bring the result to a clinician and talk through what it means.

Will a positive screening show up on my medical record?

The screening result is usually recorded in your chart like any other test. A screening score is not a diagnosis. A diagnosis is added only if a clinician confirms one after a fuller evaluation.

Can I refuse a mental health screening?

Yes. Screening is voluntary, and you can skip the form or any question on it. If you are worried about how the answers will be used, ask your clinician before you fill it out.

Does a low score mean I am fine?

Not always. Screening tools miss some people, and a score only reflects the past 2 weeks. If you still feel something is wrong, tell your clinician and ask for a longer conversation.

Do pediatricians screen teenagers without a parent in the room?

Many pediatric offices give teens a chance to answer screening questions privately. Confidentiality rules vary by state and by the type of concern. Ask the office how it handles results, especially if there are safety concerns.
