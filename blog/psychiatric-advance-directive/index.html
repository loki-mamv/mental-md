What Is a Psychiatric Advance Directive, and How Do I Make One?

The short answer: A psychiatric advance directive (PAD) is a legal document you write while you are well. It records the mental health treatment you want and refuse during a future crisis, and it can name an agent to speak for you. Twenty-five states have a specific PAD statute, and almost all states allow some version through general advance directive law. A court commitment order or a true emergency can still override parts of it.

What is a psychiatric advance directive?

The National Resource Center on Psychiatric Advance Directives (NRC-PAD) defines a PAD as "a legal document that documents a person's preferences for future mental health treatment, and allows appointment of a health proxy to interpret those preferences during a crisis."

Most PADs have two parts:

  • Written instructions. These say what you want during an acute episode: medications you accept, medications you refuse and why, hospitals you prefer, de-escalation methods that work, and who to call about children, pets, or a job.
  • A named agent. This person decides for you if you lose capacity. Some states call the role a health care proxy or attorney-in-fact for mental health treatment.

A PAD is not a refusal of all care. In a randomized trial at Duke University, most participants used the document to refuse some medications and to name specific hospitals. None refused all treatment, and psychiatrists rated the directives as highly consistent with community practice standards.

How is a PAD different from a regular advance directive?

MedlinePlus describes advance directives as legal documents that "allow you to spell out your decisions about end-of-life care ahead of time," usually a living will plus a durable power of attorney for health care.

A psychiatric crisis is a different problem. You may be physically healthy. You may lose decision-making capacity for days or weeks, then get it back. A living will written around ventilators says nothing useful about antipsychotics, seclusion, or which hospital you trust.

NRC-PAD notes that "almost all states permit some form of legal advance directive (AD) for healthcare, which can be used to direct at least some forms of psychiatric treatment." The gap is in the detail. A general health care power of attorney may not clearly give your agent authority over psychiatric admission or medication. A PAD statute spells it out.

How do I make a psychiatric advance directive?

NRC-PAD reports that twenty-five states have adopted a specific psychiatric advance directive statute. The rest rely on general advance directive law. Start at the state-by-state page and download the form for the state where you get care.

Execution rules vary, and getting them wrong can void the document. NRC-PAD says "most states require the PAD form to be witnessed and formally signed, and some states require it be notarized." Two real examples:

  • Washington. Under RCW 71.32, the directive must be dated and signed by you, and either notarized or witnessed in writing by at least two adults. Your agent cannot be a witness, and neither can a provider involved in your care or anyone who would benefit financially.
  • North Carolina. The advance instruction for mental health treatment, at General Statutes 122C-71 through 122C-79, requires two witnesses who attest that you are competent at the time, plus a notary certification.

Capacity at signing matters. Washington law presumes an adult has capacity, and only a person with capacity may execute a directive. Write yours during a stable period, not during an admission.

Do not do it alone. In the Duke trial, 61% of participants who got one structured facilitation session completed a directive or named a proxy, compared with 3% who received written information and a referral. Families may also want our guide on how to help someone who refuses mental health treatment, because a PAD is often easier to discuss than hospitalization.

Can doctors override a psychiatric advance directive?

Yes, in defined situations. This is the part people most often misunderstand. RCW 71.32.150 in Washington lets a provider decline to follow a provision when:

  • Compliance would violate the accepted standard of care.
  • The requested treatment is not available.
  • Compliance would violate applicable law.
  • It is an emergency and compliance would endanger any person's life or health.

Involuntary commitment is the biggest limit. NRC-PAD puts it plainly: "involuntary commitment to a treatment facility takes priority over what your PAD says about hospitalization." Washington's statute says provisions that conflict with the commitment's purpose or with a court order are invalid for its duration, while the rest stay advisory. North Carolina's rules are similar, and also carve out life-threatening situations.

Revocation rules vary too. In Washington, a person with capacity may revoke a directive in writing at any time. An incapacitated principal may revoke only if he or she chose that option when signing.

Do psychiatric advance directives reduce forced treatment?

The evidence is mixed, and it depends on how the document is made and used.

The strongest positive result is the DAiP trial, published in JAMA Psychiatry in 2022. Researchers randomized 394 adults at seven French facilities. All had schizophrenia, bipolar I disorder, or schizoaffective disorder, plus a compulsory admission in the previous 12 months. One group filled out a PAD with a trained peer worker who helped share it with relatives and psychiatrists. At 12 months, 27.0% of that group had a compulsory admission, compared with 39.9% of the control group (risk difference -0.13; 95% CI, -0.22 to -0.04; P = .007). They also scored higher on empowerment and recovery. If bipolar I disorder is part of your picture, bipolar.md goes deeper.

The contrast is the CRIMSON trial, published in The Lancet in 2013. It randomized 569 people with psychosis in England to Joint Crisis Plans or usual treatment. There was no significant effect on compulsory admission: 20% in the control group and 18% in the plan group (odds ratio 0.90; 95% CI, 0.58 to 1.39; p=0.63). The authors reported that the plans were not fully implemented at all sites.

Read together, the trials point one direction. A form in a drawer does little. A document built with real facilitation, shared with the team, and opened during a crisis is what moves the numbers. Uptake is still low: a survey of 1,011 psychiatric outpatients in five U.S. cities found that only 4% to 13% had completed a PAD, while 66% to 77% wanted one if someone helped them.

Where should I keep my PAD so clinicians can find it?

A directive nobody can find in an emergency department at 2 a.m. is not doing its job. NRC-PAD advises that you "give it to your mental health care provider who will make it a part of your medical record," and "keep copies of your PAD, or your PAD registration card, on your person at all times."

A workable distribution list:

  1. Your prescriber and therapist, with a request that it be scanned into the electronic health record where emergency staff can see it.
  2. Your named agent, who holds the original or a certified copy.
  3. One or two family members or friends likely to be present in a crisis.
  4. Your wallet or phone, as a registration card or a photo of the signed pages.

Review it after every hospital stay and every medication change. If your PAD names a preferred level of care, it helps to know what those levels are, including what a partial hospitalization program involves.

A PAD is planning, not crisis help. If you or someone you know is in crisis right now, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. It is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day, every day.

The bottom line

A psychiatric advance directive keeps your own voice in the room during an episode when you cannot speak for yourself. Write it while you are well, sign it the way your state requires, name an agent who will answer the phone, and put a copy everywhere it might be needed. Expect it to guide care rather than guarantee it, because commitment law and real emergencies still take priority.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a lawyer to make a psychiatric advance directive?

Usually no. Most state forms are written so people can complete them on their own, and NRC-PAD publishes forms and instructions by state. Help still improves the odds: in the Duke randomized trial, 61% of people who got one structured facilitation session completed a directive, versus 3% who only received written information. A peer specialist, case manager, or attorney can help if your situation is complicated.

Can I use a PAD to refuse all psychiatric treatment?

You can refuse specific treatments, but refusal is not absolute. In the Duke trial, participants commonly refused particular medications and named hospitals they wanted to avoid, and none refused all treatment. Providers may also decline instructions that conflict with the accepted standard of care, that request unavailable treatment, or that conflict with a court commitment order.

What happens to my directive if I move to another state?

State laws differ in execution requirements and in how they handle out-of-state documents. Twenty-five states have a specific PAD statute and the others rely on general advance directive law, so the form you signed may not match your new state's rules. Check the state page on nrc-pad.org and consider signing a fresh form where you now live.

Can the agent I name admit me to a hospital against my will?

That depends on your state's statute and on what you wrote on the form. Agent powers over psychiatric admission are written differently from state to state, and no directive can override a court order for involuntary commitment. Read the admission section of your state's form closely, and talk with the person before you name them.

How often should I update my psychiatric advance directive?

No law sets a review schedule. A practical habit is to reread it after every hospital stay, after a medication change, and after you move or change treatment teams. In Washington, a person with capacity may revoke or change a directive in writing at any time. Tell your agent and your treatment team whenever you replace an older version.
