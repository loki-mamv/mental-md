How Do I Help Someone Who Refuses Mental Health Treatment?

September 13, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: You usually cannot force an adult into mental health treatment, but you can make it more likely they accept help. Start by understanding why they refuse, then listen more than you argue and look for goals you share. Get support for yourself, such as NAMI's free Family-to-Family class. If the person is in danger, call or text 988, or call 911 if someone's life is at immediate risk.

Why do people refuse mental health treatment?

Refusal rarely has one cause. Knowing the likely reason helps you choose what to say.

  • Cost. In federal survey data, not being able to afford care was the most common reason adults with an unmet need did not get services, according to the SAMHSA National Survey on Drug Use and Health. Our guide to therapy costs without insurance lists lower-cost options.
  • Stigma and fear. Some people worry about being labeled, losing a job, or being hospitalized. See our overview of mental health stigma.
  • Bad past experiences. Side effects, a poor fit with a therapist, or a traumatic hospital stay can make someone wary.
  • Not seeing the illness at all. This is called anosognosia, and it is different from denial.

What is anosognosia, and why does it matter?

Anosognosia means a person cannot perceive that they are ill. It is a symptom of the illness, not stubbornness. A 2023 research summary from the Treatment Advocacy Center explains the difference. A person in denial knows something has changed but rejects the word "illness." A person with anosognosia "typically has no idea" that anything has changed.

The same summary gives these estimates:

  • Anosognosia is present to some extent in about 60% of people with schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders.
  • It is present in about 50% of people with bipolar disorder.
  • About 30% of people with schizophrenia and 20% of people with bipolar disorder have severe anosognosia, with no awareness of their illness or symptoms.

The summary calls anosognosia "the most common reason for treatment refusal and treatment dropouts" in severe mental illness. Insight can also come and go, especially in bipolar disorder. If your loved one has bipolar disorder, bipolar.md covers the condition in more depth.

This matters for your approach. If the person truly cannot see the illness, more facts and arguments will not change their mind. They often damage trust instead.

How should you talk to someone who refuses help?

Two related approaches have some evidence behind them. Both put the relationship ahead of winning the argument.

LEAP (Listen, Empathize, Agree, Partner). Psychologist Xavier Amador developed LEAP for families of people with anosognosia. The steps are:

  1. Listen to the person's own view of what is happening, without correcting it.
  2. Empathize with the feelings behind it, such as fear, frustration, or wanting to be left alone.
  3. Agree on whatever you can, even small things like "you want fewer fights at home."
  4. Partner on a shared goal, such as sleeping better or keeping a job, that treatment might help with.

Be realistic about the evidence. The LEAP Institute's research page lists one randomized trial of LEAP itself. It included 54 people with schizophrenia after hospital discharge. The method improved insight, motivation, and adherence compared with a control therapy. The authors noted limits: the senior author was the only therapist, and fidelity was not measured.

Motivational interviewing. This clinical style helps people explore their own mixed feelings about change instead of being told what to do. A systematic review in the American Journal of Psychiatry looked at 39 studies of programs to improve medication adherence in schizophrenia. Only 13 found significant effects. Programs that used concrete problem solving or motivational techniques did better. Education alone, without behavioral parts, usually did not work.

Practical tips drawn from both approaches:

  • Pick a calm moment, not the middle of a crisis or a fight.
  • Use "I" statements: "I've noticed you're not sleeping, and I'm worried."
  • Ask what they want, then connect help to that goal.
  • Offer choices, such as a primary care visit instead of a psychiatrist, or a different type of therapy.
  • Expect many conversations. Trust builds slowly.

What can families legally do?

Most adults have the right to refuse treatment. Laws allow exceptions only in narrow cases, and the rules differ by state. This section is general information, not legal advice.

SAMHSA's report Civil Commitment and the Mental Health Care Continuum says every state allows civil commitment for people who meet its legal standard. It notes that "these standards and the procedures that are followed to impose commitment vary from state to state." Common grounds include:

  • Danger to self or others because of a mental illness.
  • Grave disability, usually defined as being unable to provide for basic needs such as food, clothing, or shelter.
  • Serious deterioration, in some states, when a person will predictably become dangerous without treatment.

Most states also have assisted outpatient treatment (AOT). This is a court order for community-based treatment for people who meet specific criteria. The Treatment Advocacy Center says AOT is authorized in nearly every state and that many states let family members petition for an AOT order. It also notes that many areas still lack working AOT programs.

To learn your state's rules, contact your local NAMI affiliate, your county mental health department, or a lawyer. The Treatment Advocacy Center also lists laws by state.

How can NAMI help families?

NAMI Family-to-Family is a free, 8-session class for family, partners, and friends of people with mental health conditions. Trained family members who have been through it teach the class.

A randomized trial in Psychiatric Services enrolled 318 family members in five Maryland counties. Compared with people on a waitlist, those who took the course had greater gains in empowerment and knowledge about the illness. Exploratory analyses also found more acceptance, less distress, and better problem solving. The course did not reduce the family members' sense of burden.

NAMI also runs family support groups and a HelpLine. You can call 800-950-6264 or text "NAMI" to 62640.

When should you call 988 or 911?

You can contact 988 for someone else. The SAMHSA 988 fact sheet says 988 counselors support "family, friends, caregivers, and advocates" who reach out about another person. Call or text 988 if your loved one talks about suicide, seems out of touch with reality, or you do not know what to do next.

For most people, the 988 counselor can help calm things down on the call. If there is an immediate physical safety threat, the counselor will contact 911. Call 911 yourself if someone has a weapon, has already hurt themselves, or is in immediate danger. When you call, say it is a mental health crisis and ask if a crisis-trained officer is available.

The 988 Lifeline's guidance for helpers says to ask directly about suicide and listen without judgment. It also says to remove means like weapons or pills. It warns: "Don't be sworn to secrecy."

The bottom line

Helping someone who refuses treatment is usually slow work. Find out why they refuse, because cost, stigma, and anosognosia each need a different approach. Listen, find shared goals, and offer choices instead of ultimatums. Learn your state's legal options before a crisis, and take care of yourself with support like NAMI Family-to-Family. If safety is at risk, call or text 988, or call 911 in an emergency.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I force an adult family member to see a psychiatrist?

In most cases, no. Adults generally have the right to refuse care unless they meet their state's legal standard for involuntary evaluation or commitment, such as being a danger to themselves or others. Rules differ by state, so check with your county mental health department, local NAMI affiliate, or an attorney.

Is anosognosia the same as being in denial?

No. A person in denial usually knows something has changed but does not want to call it an illness. A person with anosognosia often cannot perceive the change at all, because the illness affects self-awareness. Arguing with facts rarely helps in either case, but it is especially unhelpful with anosognosia.

What should I avoid saying to someone who won't get help?

Avoid labels, threats, and ultimatums you will not follow through on. Do not try to prove the person is sick, because it tends to damage trust. Instead, describe what you have noticed, say you are worried, and ask what they want for their own life.

How do I take care of myself while supporting a loved one?

Set limits on what you can do and keep up your own routines and relationships. Family programs such as NAMI Family-to-Family and NAMI family support groups are free and connect you with people in the same situation. Talking with your own therapist can also help.

Will 988 send police if I call about my family member?

Usually not. SAMHSA says most 988 contacts are handled on the call, text, or chat. If there is an immediate physical safety threat, the counselor will contact 911.
