How Does Sleep Affect Mental Health?

October 4, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents
  1. How much sleep do adults need?

  3. Does insomnia predict depression and anxiety?
  4. Does treating a sleep problem improve mental health?
  5. How does sleep loss affect bipolar disorder?
  6. When should you see a clinician about sleep?

The short answer: Sleep and mental health affect each other in both directions. Poor sleep is a symptom of many mental health conditions, and it also raises the odds of developing one later. In a 2019 meta-analysis, people with insomnia had about 2.8 times the odds of later depression and 3.2 times the odds of later anxiety. Trials also show that mental health improves when sleep improves.

How much sleep do adults need?

Most adults need at least 7 hours a night. The CDC recommends 7 or more hours for adults aged 18 to 60, 7 to 9 hours for ages 61 to 64, and 7 to 8 hours for ages 65 and older.

That number comes from a 2015 joint consensus statement from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society. The panel wrote that adults should sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis. It linked regular sleep of less than 7 hours with several health problems. Depression is on that list, next to weight gain, diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, and stroke.

Many people fall short. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) reports that about 1 in 3 U.S. adults say they do not get enough rest or sleep every day.

Hours are only one part of the picture. NHLBI uses the wider term "sleep deficiency." It covers too little sleep, sleep at the wrong time of day, poor-quality sleep, and sleep disorders.

What does poor sleep do to mood and thinking?

The effects show up the next day. NHLBI says that sleep deficiency changes activity in some parts of the brain. A person who is sleep deficient may have trouble with these tasks:

  • Making decisions and solving problems
  • Controlling emotions and behavior
  • Coping with change
  • Paying attention and remembering things

Social situations also get harder. NHLBI notes that sleep deficiency can make a person feel frustrated, cranky, or worried around other people. It can also make it harder to judge other people's emotions and reactions.

The longer-term picture is more serious. NHLBI states that sleep deficiency is linked to depression, suicide, and risk-taking behavior. A link is not proof of cause. The next two sections show what the stronger studies found.

Does insomnia predict depression and anxiety?

Yes. The best evidence comes from studies that follow people over time. These studies start with people who have insomnia but no mental disorder, then count who develops one.

A 2019 meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews pooled 13 such studies. Each one followed people for at least 12 months. Insomnia at the start predicted later:

  • Depression: odds ratio 2.83 (95% CI 1.55 to 5.17), from 10 studies
  • Anxiety: odds ratio 3.23 (95% CI 1.52 to 6.85), from 6 studies
  • Alcohol abuse: odds ratio 1.35, from 2 studies
  • Psychosis: odds ratio 1.28, from 1 study

An earlier 2011 meta-analysis looked only at depression. It pooled 21 studies and found an odds ratio of 2.60. The authors concluded that non-depressed people with insomnia have about twice the risk of developing depression.

These numbers have limits. The confidence intervals are wide, so the true effect could be smaller or larger. The 2019 authors rated the risk of bias in the studies as moderate. The 2011 authors noted that the studies did not always account for other factors. Insomnia predicts later illness, but these studies alone cannot prove that it causes it.

Does treating a sleep problem improve mental health?

The trial evidence says yes. A 2021 meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials tested the question directly. It included 65 trials with 8,608 participants. Each trial used a treatment that improved sleep, then measured mental health.

Better sleep led to these changes:

  • A medium-sized improvement in overall mental health (effect size g = -0.53)
  • A medium-sized drop in depression (g = -0.63)
  • A medium-sized drop in anxiety (g = -0.51)
  • A medium-sized drop in rumination (g = -0.49)
  • A small-to-medium drop in stress (g = -0.42)

The authors also found a dose-response pattern. Larger gains in sleep quality went with larger gains in mental health. They concluded that sleep is causally related to mental health difficulties.

This matters for care. A sleep problem is worth treatment in its own right, even when depression or anxiety is the main diagnosis. Cognitive behavioral therapy adapted for insomnia is one common approach, and insomnia.md covers insomnia treatment in more detail. Our guide to the main types of therapy explains how cognitive behavioral therapy works.

How does sleep loss affect bipolar disorder?

Sleep and bipolar disorder are closely tied. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) lists a decreased need for sleep as a symptom of a manic episode. It lists trouble falling asleep, early waking, or too much sleep as symptoms of a depressive episode.

Sleep loss can also come before an episode. A 2017 study in the British Journal of Psychiatry interviewed 3,140 people with bipolar disorder. It asked if sleep loss had triggered their episodes of high or low mood. Sleep loss that triggered high mood was more likely in people with bipolar I disorder (odds ratio 2.81) and in women (odds ratio 1.43).

The study relied on what people remembered, which is a weakness. The authors still advised care in situations that disrupt sleep, such as shift work and international travel.

NIMH gives similar practical advice. It tells people with bipolar disorder to keep a routine for eating, sleeping, and exercise. It also notes that cognitive behavioral therapy adapted for insomnia can help as part of treatment for bipolar depression. For more on the condition, see bipolar.md.

When should you see a clinician about sleep?

The CDC's advice is simple: talk to a healthcare provider if you have problems sleeping. These signs make a visit more important:

  • The problem is frequent and lasting. NHLBI says that insomnia is chronic when it occurs 3 or more nights a week for 3 months or longer.
  • You sleep a lot but stay tired. NHLBI advises a visit if you sleep more than 8 hours a night and do not feel well rested.
  • You snore loudly or wake up gasping. Clinicians ask about these because they can point to sleep apnea.
  • Your mood has changed too. Low mood, loss of interest, or constant worry together with poor sleep needs an assessment. A mental health screening is a common first step.
  • You need much less sleep and feel wired. In a person with bipolar disorder, this can be a sign of a manic episode.

NHLBI suggests that you keep a sleep diary for 1 to 2 weeks before the visit. Write down when you go to bed, wake up, and nap. Also record caffeine, alcohol, and exercise. Our guide on when to seek therapy covers the mental health side of that decision.

If you or someone you know is thinking about suicide, call or text the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline at 988. In a life-threatening situation, call 911.

The bottom line

Poor sleep is a symptom of mental health problems, and it can also contribute to them. Adults need 7 or more hours a night. Insomnia roughly doubles to triples the odds of later depression and anxiety in long-term studies. Trials show that treatment for a sleep problem also improves depression, anxiety, and stress. For people with bipolar disorder, a regular sleep routine is part of staying well. A sleep problem that lasts for weeks is a good reason to see a clinician.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can lack of sleep cause depression?

It can raise the risk. A 2019 meta-analysis of long-term studies found that people with insomnia had about 2.8 times the odds of later depression. A 2021 meta-analysis of 65 trials found that depression symptoms dropped when sleep improved, which supports a causal link.

Is 6 hours of sleep enough for an adult?

For most adults, no. The CDC and a joint statement from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend 7 or more hours a night. The statement links regular sleep of less than 7 hours with health problems that include depression.

Which comes first, the sleep problem or the mental health problem?

It can go either way. Poor sleep is a symptom of depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder. Long-term studies also show that insomnia in people with no mental disorder predicts a later one.

Can one bad night of sleep trigger a manic episode?

Sleep loss is a reported trigger for high mood in bipolar disorder. In a 2017 study of 3,140 people with bipolar disorder, this trigger was more likely in people with bipolar I disorder and in women. People with bipolar disorder should tell their clinician about any sudden change in sleep.

What is chronic insomnia?

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute says insomnia is chronic when it occurs 3 or more nights a week and lasts 3 months or longer. A clinician can check for causes such as another sleep disorder, a medicine, or a mental health condition.
