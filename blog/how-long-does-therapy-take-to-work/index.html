How Long Does Therapy Take to Work?

Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Most people notice a change within the first 8 sessions, and most who will improve have done so by about 26. The landmark dose-effect analysis found that about 50% of patients measurably improved by session 8 and about 75% by session 26. Structured therapies such as CBT, ERP and prolonged exposure are built to run 8 to 20 sessions. If you feel no different after 8 to 10 sessions, that is the point to talk with your therapist about changing the plan.

How many sessions before most people feel better?

The best-known answer comes from Howard and colleagues (1986). They pooled 15 samples covering 2,431 outpatients in once-weekly psychotherapy. Their probit analysis produced a curve that still shapes how clinics plan treatment.

  • 10% to 18% of patients showed some improvement before the first session, simply from making the appointment.
  • By 8 sessions, 48% to 58% had measurably improved. The authors rounded this to about 50%.
  • By 26 sessions (six months of weekly therapy), about 75% had improved.
  • By a year of treatment, about 85% had improved.

The curve flattens as sessions add up. Early sessions produce the largest gains, and later sessions add smaller amounts. For anxiety and depression, 50% of patients improved within 8 to 13 sessions. For more complex conditions, the same milestone took 13 to 26 sessions or longer.

Newer work sets a higher bar. The American Psychological Association states that 15 to 20 sessions are needed for 50% of patients to recover on self-report symptom measures. "Improve" and "recover" are different finish lines. A review indexed by the NIH National Library of Medicine found that trial patients received a mean of 12.7 sessions, and 57.6% achieved a clinically significant response.

How many sessions does each type of therapy take?

Structured therapies come with a planned session count. The UK's NICE depression guideline (NG222) lists typical courses, and adds that people with other health problems may need more sessions.

  • Individual CBT for depression: usually 16 sessions for more severe depression, and about 8 for less severe depression.
  • Behavioural activation: 12 to 16 sessions for more severe depression.
  • Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT): 8 to 16 sessions, usually 16 for more severe depression.
  • Problem-solving therapy: 6 to 12 sessions.
  • Guided self-help: 6 to 8 structured sessions with a practitioner.
  • Exposure and response prevention (ERP) for OCD: 12 to 20 sessions of about one hour, per the International OCD Foundation. The first 2 to 3 sessions cover education and assessment.
  • Prolonged exposure for PTSD: 8 to 15 weekly sessions of 60 to 90 minutes, per the VA National Center for PTSD.
  • EMDR for PTSD: about 3 months of weekly 50 to 90 minute sessions, per the VA National Center for PTSD.

Open-ended talk therapy has no set endpoint. Psychodynamic and supportive therapy can run for months or years. The APA notes that treatments focused on a specific problem are generally briefer than therapies with a broader focus. For a comparison of these approaches, see our guide to the types of therapy compared.

What should "working" look like at week 2, 4 and 8?

Progress rarely feels like a straight line. Howard's team found that patients begin to feel better before they appear better to clinicians. Subjective relief comes first. Measurable change in behavior and function follows. Here is a realistic timeline for weekly sessions.

  • Week 2: You should feel understood and have a clear goal. You may feel a small lift from taking action. SAMHSA notes that some people know after the first few appointments that the fit is wrong. That is normal, and worth raising early.
  • Week 4: Roughly the point where early responders separate from the rest. Lambert (2005) describes an "early response phenomenon" in which a sizable share of patients improve within the first few sessions. The VA says many people in prolonged exposure or EMDR start to feel better "after a few sessions."
  • Week 8: The 50% milestone. About half of people have measurably improved by now. In depression trials, a 2021 meta-analysis of 228 trials found a 41% response rate (50% symptom reduction) at about 2 months. Roughly one third of patients had remitted. Only 5% got worse, compared with 12% to 13% in control groups.

Ask your therapist to track symptoms with a short scale such as the PHQ-9 or GAD-7. NICE recommends routine outcome monitoring at each session. Numbers make "is this working?" a question with an answer.

Does more therapy always mean better results?

Not by itself. Cuijpers and colleagues (2013) analyzed 70 studies with 5,403 depressed patients. The link between number of sessions and effect size was small and vanished after adjustment. Total contact time did not matter either. What did matter was frequency: moving from one to two sessions per week raised the effect size by g=0.45 with the same total sessions.

A 2024 update covering 176 studies and 15,158 participants found the same pattern. More sessions did not predict larger effects. Each extra week of treatment length was linked to a slightly smaller effect. Twice-weekly sessions were again linked to larger effects. The authors concluded that therapy should be brief and, when more sessions are needed, delivered closer together.

The practical lesson: twelve weekly sessions over three months likely beat twelve sessions spread across a year. Consistency counts more than the raw total.

What slows therapy down?

Several factors push people to the slow end of the curve.

  • Chronic or complex problems. The APA notes acute difficulties usually need fewer sessions than chronic conditions. Howard's data showed the 50% milestone arriving at 13 to 26 sessions for the most complex cases.
  • Other conditions at the same time. NICE flags that people with comorbid mental or physical health problems often need additional sessions. Depression alongside anxiety, insomnia or chronic pain takes longer to treat.
  • Gaps between sessions. The Cuijpers data show that stretching sessions out weakens the effect. Missed weeks reset momentum.
  • Skipped homework. CBT, ERP and behavioural activation all rely on practice between sessions. NICE lists willingness to complete homework as a requirement for these therapies.
  • Cost and access. People who cannot afford weekly sessions often space them out. Our guide to therapy costs without insurance covers sliding-scale and lower-cost options.

When should you change therapists or approaches?

Use the 8-session benchmark as a checkpoint, not a deadline. The National Institute of Mental Health puts it plainly: if you have been in therapy for a reasonable amount of time and are not getting better, talk to your therapist. You might want to explore other professionals or approaches.

Signs that a change is worth discussing:

  1. No measurable change on a symptom scale after 8 to 10 weekly sessions.
  2. No clear treatment goal or plan after the first 2 to 3 sessions.
  3. Your therapist does not use an evidence-based method for your diagnosis, such as ERP for OCD or exposure-based therapy for PTSD.
  4. You leave sessions feeling judged, dismissed or unsafe.
  5. Symptoms are getting worse, or you have thoughts of harming yourself. Call or text 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline) right away.

Changing therapists does not mean starting from zero. Your notes, diagnosis and what you learned come with you. Sometimes the fix is adding a medication evaluation rather than switching therapists. Our guide to psychiatrists vs psychologists vs therapists explains who does what.

The bottom line

Expect early relief within a few sessions, a measurable change by session 8, and a solid result by session 12 to 20 for structured therapies. Half of people improve by 8 sessions and three quarters by 26. Weekly or twice-weekly sessions work better than the same number spread thin. Track symptoms with a scale, and if nothing has moved after 8 to 10 sessions, raise it with your therapist and consider a different approach.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal to feel worse after the first few therapy sessions?

Yes, for some people. Talking about painful material and starting exposure exercises can raise distress before it falls. In depression trials, only about 5% of people in therapy got clinically worse, compared with 12% to 13% in control groups. Tell your therapist if the worsening lasts more than a couple of weeks.

Can therapy work in just a few sessions?

It can for a specific, recent problem. Problem-solving therapy runs 6 to 12 sessions, and guided self-help runs 6 to 8. Howard's data also showed 10% to 18% of people improve before the first session simply from booking it. Chronic or complex conditions usually need a longer course.

Is once a week enough, or should I go more often?

Weekly is the standard and works for most people. Meta-analyses by Cuijpers and colleagues found that twice-weekly sessions produced larger effects than once-weekly with the same total number of sessions. If you can afford it and your therapist agrees, front-loading sessions may speed things up.

How do I know if therapy is working if I still have bad days?

Look at the trend, not single days. A symptom scale such as the PHQ-9 or GAD-7 completed every session shows whether scores are falling over weeks. Also watch function: sleeping better, doing more, avoiding less. Those often change before mood does.

Does online therapy take longer to work than in-person therapy?

The evidence does not show a slower timeline for video-based therapy when the same method and session count are used. What matters more is the type of therapy, the frequency of sessions, and doing the between-session work. See our article comparing online and in-person therapy for the trial data.
