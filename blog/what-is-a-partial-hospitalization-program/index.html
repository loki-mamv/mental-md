What Is a Partial Hospitalization Program (PHP) and Who Needs One?

The short answer: A partial hospitalization program (PHP) is structured psychiatric treatment you attend during the day, then go home at night. It is the most intensive level of outpatient care. Medicare requires a plan of care with at least 20 hours of therapy a week. PHP fits people who just left a psychiatric hospital, or who would need a hospital stay without it, but who are safe to sleep at home.

What happens in a partial hospitalization program?

A PHP is a day program run by a hospital outpatient department or a community mental health center. You spend most of the day in treatment, then return home. Medicare.gov says these programs usually include 4 to 8 hours of care each day, with no overnight stay.

The federal rule for Medicare, 42 CFR 410.43, lists the kinds of services a PHP can include:

  • Individual and group therapy with physicians, psychologists, or other licensed mental health professionals
  • Occupational therapy and activity therapies that are not just recreation
  • Psychiatric nursing and social work services
  • Medications given as part of treatment
  • Family counseling focused on the patient's treatment
  • Patient education and diagnostic services

A typical day mixes group sessions, skills work, and a check-in with a psychiatrist or nurse about symptoms and medication. The team writes a plan of care with specific goals. If you are not sure which kind of therapist does what, see our guide to psychiatrists, psychologists, and therapists.

How is PHP different from inpatient care and IOP?

Mental health care runs on a ladder of intensity. PHP sits between a hospital stay and a lighter outpatient program.

  • Inpatient care: You stay in the hospital around the clock. This is for people who need 24-hour supervision, often for safety.
  • Partial hospitalization (PHP): Medicare requires a plan of care with at least 20 hours of therapeutic services per week. You sleep at home.
  • Intensive outpatient (IOP): Under 42 CFR 410.44, Medicare IOP requires at least 9 hours of therapeutic services per week. That usually means shorter days or fewer days than PHP.
  • Standard outpatient therapy: Usually one session a week or less with a therapist or prescriber.

The biggest difference between PHP and IOP is the entry bar. For PHP, a provider must certify that you would otherwise need inpatient psychiatric care. The IOP rule does not require that. Both rules say the person must not need 24-hour care and must not be judged dangerous to self or others. For a wider look at treatment styles, see types of therapy compared.

Who is a good fit for a PHP?

A PHP usually serves one of two groups. A Medicare coverage policy (LCD L33626) describes them this way:

  1. Step-down patients. People leaving an inpatient psychiatric unit, where the PHP takes the place of more days in the hospital.
  2. Step-up patients. People whose symptoms are getting worse and who would otherwise need to be admitted.

The federal rule adds more criteria. The person should need a coordinated program, not isolated sessions. They should have enough support at home when they are not at the program. They need a mental health or substance use disorder diagnosis. They also need the mental and emotional ability to take part in active treatment.

In practice, PHP often helps people with severe depression, bipolar disorder episodes, anxiety or OCD that has stopped daily life, eating disorders, or trauma symptoms after a crisis. Condition-specific guides are on bipolar.md and our other sister sites. PHP is not the right place for someone at immediate risk of suicide or violence. That person needs emergency or inpatient care first.

Does PHP work as well as a hospital stay?

For many people, the evidence says yes. A Cochrane review looked at 10 randomized trials with 2,685 people. It compared acute day hospital care with inpatient care for adults with acute psychiatric disorders.

The review found no difference in readmission after discharge, and likely no difference in quality of life or treatment satisfaction. The authors concluded that acute day hospital care is as effective as inpatient care for these patients. One trade-off: the day hospital episode tended to last longer than the hospital stay. The trials excluded people who were mostly under 18 or over 65, or whose main problem was substance use.

Does Medicare cover partial hospitalization?

Yes. Medicare Part B covers PHP through a hospital outpatient department or a community mental health center. Medicare.gov lists the main conditions:

  • Your provider certifies that you would otherwise need inpatient treatment.
  • Your care plan states that you need at least 20 hours of therapeutic services each week.
  • You pay a percentage of the Medicare-approved amount for each professional service, plus coinsurance for each PHP day, after the Part B deductible.

Medicare does not pay for meals or transportation to the program. The paperwork also has deadlines. Under 42 CFR 424.24, a physician must recertify the need for PHP by the 18th day of services, then at least every 30 days after that.

If you need fewer hours, Medicare also covers intensive outpatient program services. Your care plan must call for at least 9 hours a week, and you pay 20% of the approved amount after the deductible. Private insurance and Medicaid rules vary, so call your plan and ask whether it needs prior authorization. If cost is the barrier, our article on therapy costs without insurance may help.

How do you find a partial hospitalization program?

Most people enter a PHP through a referral. The easiest paths:

  • Ask the inpatient team before discharge. Hospital social workers often arrange a PHP as the next step.
  • Ask your psychiatrist, therapist, or primary care doctor. They can tell you whether your symptoms fit PHP or IOP.
  • Search FindTreatment.gov. This is SAMHSA's confidential, anonymous locator for mental health and substance use treatment. You can filter by treatment type, services, and payment accepted.
  • Call your insurer. Ask for in-network PHP providers and any authorization steps.

If things feel urgent, contact the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. You can call or text 988, or chat online. It is free, confidential, and open 24/7. Counselors can talk through the crisis and point you to local care. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.

The bottom line

A partial hospitalization program gives hospital-level structure without an overnight stay. Medicare ties it to at least 20 hours of therapy a week and to a provider's statement that you would otherwise need inpatient care. It fits people stepping down from the hospital or trying to avoid admission, as long as they are safe at home. Research shows acute day hospital care can work as well as inpatient care for many adults. Ask your care team, search FindTreatment.gov, or reach 988 if you are in crisis.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a partial hospitalization program last?

Length depends on how quickly symptoms improve and on your plan of care. Medicare requires a physician to recertify the need for PHP by the 18th day of services, then at least every 30 days. Many programs step people down to intensive outpatient care once they are more stable.

Can I work or go to school while in a PHP?

It is usually hard, because a PHP takes up most of the day. Medicare.gov says programs usually run 4 to 8 hours a day. Many people take medical leave, then return to work or school while in an intensive outpatient program.

Do I sleep at home during partial hospitalization?

Yes. You attend treatment during the day and go home at night. That is why the program expects you to have adequate support outside program hours and not need 24-hour care.

What if a PHP is not enough?

If symptoms get worse or safety becomes a concern, the team can recommend inpatient admission. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911. For crisis support, call or text 988 at any hour.

Is PHP the same as a day program?

The terms overlap. Research often calls it acute day hospital care, and many programs use the name day treatment. For Medicare, what matters is that the program meets the partial hospitalization rules, including at least 20 hours of therapeutic services a week.
