What Is an Intensive Outpatient Program (IOP) for Mental Health?

The short answer: An intensive outpatient program (IOP) is structured mental health or substance use treatment you attend several times a week while living at home. Medicare defines it as care that needs at least 9 hours of therapeutic services a week. It sits between weekly therapy and a partial hospitalization program. Since January 1, 2024, Medicare Part B covers IOP services.

What happens in an intensive outpatient program?

An IOP gives you a coordinated team and a set schedule, not a single weekly appointment. Most of the time goes to group therapy, with individual sessions and medication check-ins added. You go home after each session and can often keep working or attending school.

The federal Medicare rule, 42 CFR 410.44, lists the services an IOP can include:

  • Individual and group therapy with physicians, psychologists, or other licensed mental health professionals
  • Occupational therapy and activity therapies that are not mainly recreational
  • Services from social workers and trained psychiatric nurses
  • Medications given as part of treatment
  • Family counseling focused on the patient's condition
  • Patient education and diagnostic services

A physician writes a plan of treatment. Under 42 CFR 424.24, that plan must state your diagnosis, the type, amount, and frequency of services, and your treatment goals.

How many hours a week does an IOP take?

The usual floor is 9 hours a week. Medicare requires a plan of care calling for at least 9 hours of therapeutic services each week. Programs spread those hours in different ways, such as a few longer sessions or several shorter ones.

Some programs run mornings, and others run evenings so people can keep a day job. Many IOPs now also offer telehealth groups. A study at Western Psychiatric Hospital in Pittsburgh compared 191 patients in an office-based IOP with 200 patients in a telehealth version during COVID-19. Depression and anxiety scores dropped in both groups, and the change scores did not differ significantly.

How is an IOP different from partial hospitalization?

Both are day programs, and you sleep at home in both. The main differences are hours and how sick you need to be to qualify.

  • Partial hospitalization (PHP): Medicare requires a plan of care with at least 20 hours of services a week. A provider must certify that you would otherwise need inpatient psychiatric care.
  • Intensive outpatient (IOP): At least 9 hours a week. Medicare.gov says you do not need to qualify for inpatient treatment first.

People often move between levels. Someone may step down from a hospital stay to a PHP, then to an IOP, then to weekly therapy. Others step up to an IOP when weekly sessions are no longer enough. For a full look at day programs, see our guide to partial hospitalization programs.

Who is a good fit for an IOP?

The Medicare rule describes the intended patient. An IOP is meant for people who:

  • Have a mental health or substance use disorder diagnosis
  • Need more than isolated outpatient sessions and are likely to benefit from a coordinated program
  • Do not need 24-hour care
  • Have enough support at home when not at the program
  • Are not judged to be dangerous to themselves or others
  • Can take part in active treatment and handle its intensity

In practice, IOPs serve adults and teens with depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, PTSD, eating disorders, and substance use disorders. Some programs focus on one condition, such as a trauma track or a dual diagnosis track. If depression is the main concern, depression.md covers its symptoms and treatments in more depth.

An IOP is not the right setting for someone in immediate danger. If you or someone near you is thinking about suicide, call or text 988, or go to the nearest emergency room.

What kinds of therapy do IOPs use?

Most IOPs build on skills-based group therapy. Common approaches include:

  • Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT): Spotting and changing unhelpful thought patterns and behaviors.
  • Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT): Skills for mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and relationships.
  • Exposure-based therapy: Used in many PTSD and OCD tracks.
  • Relapse prevention and motivational work: Standard in substance use IOPs.
  • Medication management: Regular reviews with a psychiatrist or psychiatric nurse practitioner.

The evidence for these programs is stronger in some areas than others. A 2014 review in Psychiatric Services rated the evidence for substance use IOPs as high. It found outcomes comparable to inpatient or residential care for most people. For general psychiatric IOPs, most data come from single-program studies without control groups. One study of 146 clients in DBT-informed programs found depression, anxiety, stress, and hopelessness all dropped from intake to discharge in the IOP group. How long does an IOP last?

There is no fixed length. Under the Medicare rule, the physician sets the frequency and duration of services. That decision rests on accepted medical practice and a reasonable expectation that you will improve.

Medicare also builds in check-ins. A treating physician must recertify the need for IOP at least every 60 days. The recertification must describe your response to treatment, the symptoms that still put you at risk, and your discharge goals. Private plans often review progress on their own schedule, sometimes more often. Ask the program how it decides when you are ready to step down.

Does insurance cover an IOP?

Medicare covers it now, which is new. The CMS final rule for 2024 created payment for IOP services starting January 1, 2024. It implements provisions of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023.

According to Medicare.gov, Part B covers IOP services at:

  • Hospital outpatient departments
  • Community mental health centers
  • Federally Qualified Health Centers and Rural Health Clinics
  • Opioid Treatment Programs, for opioid use disorder

After the Part B deductible, you pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount for services from professionals who accept assignment. At a hospital outpatient department or community mental health center, you also pay coinsurance for each day of IOP services.

For private coverage, HealthCare.gov says all Marketplace plans cover mental health and substance use services as essential health benefits. Parity rules also say limits on this care cannot be more restrictive than limits on medical and surgical care. Medicaid coverage varies by state. Many plans require prior authorization, so call before you enroll. If your plan network is thin, our article on finding a therapist who takes insurance explains why and what to try.

The bottom line

An IOP gives you several hours of structured treatment a week, with at least 9 hours under Medicare rules, while you live at home. It fits people who need more than weekly therapy but do not need a hospital or a full-day program. Most programs mix group therapy, individual sessions, and medication management. Medicare has covered IOP since January 2024, and most private plans cover it under parity rules, often with prior authorization.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I keep working while in an intensive outpatient program?

Often, yes. Many IOPs meet a few days a week for part of the day, and some offer evening tracks. Talk with your employer about schedule changes if you need them.

Is a virtual IOP as good as an in-person one?

Early data are encouraging. A Pittsburgh study of about 390 patients found similar drops in depression and anxiety scores in office-based and telehealth IOP. Fit still matters, so ask how the program handles safety checks and group participation online.

Do I need a referral to join an IOP?

Most programs start with an intake assessment, and many accept self-referrals. Medicare requires a physician certification and a written plan of treatment. Private plans may require prior authorization before you start.

What happens after an IOP ends?

Most people step down to standard outpatient care, such as weekly therapy and medication follow-up. Good programs plan this transition before discharge. Ask for a written aftercare plan and a first appointment date.

Are there IOPs for teenagers?

Yes. Many programs run separate adolescent tracks, often after school, and many include family sessions. Ask whether the program has staff trained in treating teens.
