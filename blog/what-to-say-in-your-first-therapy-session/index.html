What Should I Say in My First Therapy Session?

September 2, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Say why you came in and what you want to change. Those two things give the therapist enough to start. The first session is an intake, so the therapist asks most of the questions. You can answer at your own pace. You do not need a rehearsed story, and you can say that you are not ready to discuss something yet.

What happens in a first therapy session?

The first session is mostly information gathering. The American Psychological Association (APA) says it "might seem like a game of 20 questions." A typical session lasts 45 to 50 minutes. The therapist usually starts with logistics: fees, how to cancel, and confidentiality. Then they ask an opening question such as "What brought you here today?" or "What made you decide to come in now rather than a month or a year ago?"

Expect questions about your own and your family's history of depression, anxiety, or similar problems. Expect questions about sleep, appetite, and other changes in your habits. The therapist will also ask about your support system: family, friends, and coworkers. SAMHSA notes that intake calls are confidential, so you can be open about mental health, drug, or alcohol concerns.

The APA says this history-taking may take more than one session. The therapist lets you set the pace. Once they have a full picture, you build a treatment plan together. For a sense of how many sessions follow, see how long therapy takes to work.

What if I do not know where to start?

You do not need a polished story. The APA says even a vague idea of what you want to accomplish helps the therapist proceed. Try one of these openers:

  • "I have felt off for a few months and I cannot explain why."
  • "I am not sure this is a real problem, but it is affecting my sleep and my work."
  • "My doctor suggested I come in. I am nervous and I do not know what to say."
  • "Something happened last year that I still think about every day."
  • "I want to handle stress better, but I do not know what is wrong."

It is fine to name a feeling without naming a cause. The APA gives the example of feeling angry or sad without knowing why. It is also fine to say "I am not ready to talk about that yet." The APA states that a therapist should not push you to share more than you are comfortable with early on.

Bring a short list. The APA suggests writing down the points you want to cover and what you want to work on.

What should I share about goals, history, and medications?

Four topics are worth covering, even briefly, in the first one or two sessions.

  • Goals. Say what "better" would look like. Sleeping through the night, fewer panic episodes, or a decision you keep avoiding are all valid goals. The APA calls goal-setting collaborative, because both you and the therapist need to be invested in the plan.
  • History. Mention past therapy, what helped, and what did not. Include any family history of mental health conditions.
  • Medications. The APA advises writing down each medication and dose before you arrive. Include psychiatric medicines, sleep aids, and supplements. If you want to discuss medication, ask early whether this therapist can prescribe or will refer you. Our guide to psychiatrists, psychologists, and therapists explains who can prescribe.
  • Safety. If you have thoughts of harming yourself or others, say so. Therapists ask about this directly, and an honest answer helps them plan support, including a safety plan. Sharing this does not automatically end confidentiality. The next section explains the limits.

Many people start therapy for anxiety, and the intake questions are similar. See anxiety.md for symptom guides.

What does confidentiality cover, and where does it stop?

Confidentiality is built into the APA Ethics Code. Standard 4.02 requires psychologists to discuss the limits of confidentiality "at the outset of the relationship." Standard 4.05 allows disclosure without consent in only four cases: needed services, professional consultation, protection from harm, or payment. The APA says you should receive written privacy information at your first visit.

Federal law adds a floor. Under HIPAA, psychotherapy notes get special protection. HHS guidance states that a provider needs your authorization before disclosing psychotherapy notes for any reason. That includes sharing them with another treating provider. Your insurer receives diagnosis and treatment information for billing, but not your session notes.

The limits are narrow and specific. According to the APA and HHS:

  • A therapist may disclose information to protect you or the public from serious harm. An example is a plan to attempt suicide or hurt someone.
  • Therapists are mandated reporters. They must report ongoing abuse or neglect of children, older adults, or people with disabilities.
  • A court order can require release of records.
  • HHS notes that the "duty to warn" comes from professional ethics, state law, and court decisions such as Tarasoff. States vary on whether a warning is mandatory or permitted.

Having thoughts of suicide is not the same as an imminent plan. Saying you have had dark thoughts usually leads to more questions and a safety plan. It rarely leads to a call to someone else. Ask your therapist to explain exactly where their line is. Under Standard 4.02, they must tell you.

What questions should I ask the therapist?

The first session runs both ways. The APA says you are assessing the therapist while they assess you. NIMH suggests asking about credentials, specialties, treatment approach and its evidence, experience with your condition, timeline, medication, and confidentiality. NAMI lists ten questions any therapist should answer clearly by the third session. The most useful:

  1. What problems will you help me with?
  2. What is your explanation for why I have them?
  3. What is the goal of this treatment, and how will it change things?
  4. How will we monitor whether I am getting better? What measures will you use?
  5. How many sessions will this take, and how will we know when to stop?
  6. What do you expect from me between sessions?
  7. What other treatments might help, and how strong is the research for this one?

NAMI says good answers are specific. "We will figure this out together" is a weak answer to a question about your diagnosis. Different approaches suit different problems; our comparison of types of therapy covers the main ones.

Does the relationship with my therapist really matter?

Yes, and the evidence base is large. The working alliance means agreement on goals, agreement on tasks, and a bond of trust. A 2018 meta-analysis in the journal Psychotherapy by Flückiger and colleagues pooled 295 studies. They covered more than 30,000 patients and were published between 1978 and 2017. The alliance-outcome correlation for face-to-face therapy was r = 0.278, equivalent to d = 0.579. The relation held across treatment approaches, patient characteristics, countries, and outcome measures. For internet-based therapy the correlation was almost identical at r = 0.275.

This is why the first session matters beyond intake. If you feel heard and the goals make sense to you, the alliance is forming. If not, say so.

Switching is allowed. NIMH says to talk to your therapist if you are not getting better after a reasonable amount of time. It then suggests exploring other professionals or approaches. The APA notes that a therapist who feels the fit is wrong should refer you to someone else. Reasons to switch include feeling judged, unclear goals after three sessions, or a therapist who cannot explain their plan. Discomfort from hard topics is not a reason to switch. That discomfort is part of the work.

The bottom line

Open with why you came and what you want to change. Answer the intake questions as honestly as you can, including medications and safety. Ask the therapist what they think is going on and how they will track progress. Confidentiality covers nearly everything, with narrow exceptions for imminent harm, abuse reporting, and court orders. The relationship you build predicts outcomes across every therapy type studied, so pay attention to fit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to tell my therapist everything in the first session?

No. The APA says the therapist should not push you to share more than you are comfortable with until you know each other better. You can say you are not ready to talk about something yet. History-taking often takes more than one session, and you set the pace.

Will my therapist tell anyone if I say I have thought about suicide?

Usually not. Disclosure without consent is limited to serious and imminent threats of harm, per HHS and the APA Ethics Code. Saying you have had thoughts of suicide normally leads to more questions and a safety plan, not a call to family or police. Ask your therapist to explain their exact limits at the first visit.

What should I bring to my first therapy appointment?

The APA suggests a short list of what you want to cover, a written list of your medications and doses, and any records from past therapy or testing. Bring your insurance card, a photo ID, a form of payment, and your calendar to book the next session. A notebook helps you remember what was said.

Is it normal to feel drained after the first session?

Yes. The APA notes you may feel tired, emotionally spent, or distracted after a first session that covers upsetting topics. It suggests booking the appointment later in the day so you do not have to return to work right after. Feeling worn out is not a sign the session went badly.

How soon should I decide whether a therapist is a good fit?

NAMI says a therapist should give clear answers about your problem, the goal, and how progress will be measured by the third session. If the answers stay vague, or you feel judged, it is reasonable to look elsewhere. NIMH advises talking with your therapist first if you are not improving after a reasonable time.
