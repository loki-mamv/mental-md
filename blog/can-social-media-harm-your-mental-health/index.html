Can Social Media Use Harm Your Mental Health?

October 7, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: It can, but the average effect is smaller than most headlines suggest. Reviews of the research describe the link between general social media use and mental health as weak or inconsistent. The link is much more consistent for heavy or problematic use. A 2025 meta-analysis of 10 randomized trials found that cutting back reduced depressive symptoms by a small amount.

What does the research actually show?

The research shows a real association, but a modest one. A 2022 umbrella review pooled 25 reviews on social media and adolescent mental health. Most of those reviews called the associations "weak" or "inconsistent." A few called the same associations "substantial" and "deleterious." The reviewers were looking at similar data and reading it differently.

A newer umbrella review published in 2026 covered 72 reviews of people aged 10 to 24. It reached a similar split verdict:

  • General use (how much time a person spends) showed weak and inconsistent links with both well-being and ill-being.
  • Problematic use was consistently linked to ill-being.

The author added a caution. Many studies of general use were too small to detect a modest effect. Mixed findings may reflect weak study design, not proof that there is no effect.

The US Surgeon General took a similar position in a 2023 advisory. It noted that up to 95% of youth aged 13 to 17 use a social media platform. It also said that robust independent safety analyses of the effect on youth have not yet been done. In plain terms, nobody has shown that social media is safe for young people, and nobody has proven the size of the harm.

Does social media cause depression or anxiety?

Nobody has proven that it does. Most of the evidence is correlational. It shows that two things occur together, not that one causes the other.

The CDC's 2023 Youth Risk Behavior Survey is a good example. In that survey, 77.0% of US high school students used social media at least several times a day. Compared with less frequent users, these students were more likely to report:

  • Persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness (adjusted prevalence ratio 1.35)
  • Electronic bullying (1.54)
  • Bullying at school (1.31)
  • Seriously considering suicide (1.21)

A ratio of 1.35 means the outcome was about 35% more common in frequent users. The CDC authors state the limit plainly. The survey is a snapshot in time, so it cannot establish cause or direction. A teen who already feels low may spend more time online, and the survey cannot tell the difference.

Studies that follow people over time are a step stronger. A 2019 cohort study in JAMA Psychiatry followed 6,595 US adolescents. After adjustment for other factors, teens who used social media more than 3 hours a day had a higher risk of internalizing problems a year later than teens who did not use it. Internalizing problems include symptoms of depression and anxiety. The relative risk ratio was 1.60 for 3 to 6 hours a day and 1.78 for more than 6 hours. This is still an observational study, and the time online was self-reported.

Who is most at risk?

The evidence points to the pattern of use more than to one type of person. Three patterns stand out in the sources above:

  • Heavy daily use. In the JAMA Psychiatry cohort, the adjusted risk rose only above 3 hours a day.
  • Problematic use. One systematic review describes use as problematic when it starts to interfere with daily life and mental well-being. This is the pattern with the most consistent link to poor mental health.
  • Online bullying. Frequent users in the CDC survey reported more electronic bullying.

The CDC survey also found that girls were more likely than boys to be frequent users (81.8% compared with 72.9%).

What social media replaces also matters. If scrolling cuts into sleep, that has its own cost. Our guide on how sleep affects mental health covers that evidence. The same is true when online contact replaces time with people in person. See how loneliness affects mental health.

Does social media affect adults too?

Yes, although adults get less research attention than teens, and the adult studies have weak spots. The best adult evidence comes from experiments that ask people to change their use.

A 2023 systematic review looked at 23 experimental studies in adults. The results were split: 39% found improved mental well-being, 30% found mixed effects, and 30% found no effect. Depression was the outcome that improved most often. It improved in 7 of the 10 studies that measured it.

The reviewers rated 22 of the 23 studies as weak in quality. Most studies (16 of 23) recruited university students, so the results may not apply to older adults or to people with a diagnosed condition.

Does cutting back on social media help?

Cutting back seems to help mood a little. Quitting completely has weaker support.

A 2025 meta-analysis pooled 10 randomized controlled trials with 1,491 participants. Reducing or stopping social media use lowered depressive symptoms. The effect size was g = 0.25 after adjustment for publication bias. Researchers usually call an effect of that size small. The authors asked for better-designed trials and noted that long-term effects are unknown.

A second 2025 meta-analysis looked only at full abstinence. It pooled 10 studies with 4,674 participants. It found no significant effect on positive mood, negative mood, or life satisfaction. Longer breaks did not produce better results.

Two more findings are useful:

  • In the depression meta-analysis, trials that reduced use had about twice the effect of trials that required abstinence. The difference was not statistically significant, so treat it as a hint.
  • In the adult review, therapy-based programs worked more often than simple limits. Five of 6 studies that used methods such as cognitive behavioral therapy showed improvement. Only 1 of 5 studies of limited use, and 3 of 12 studies of full abstinence, did.

Taken together, the trials suggest that how a person uses social media matters more than a total ban.

When should you talk to a professional?

Talk to a clinician if low mood, worry, or sleep trouble lasts for weeks, or if it affects work, school, or relationships. This is true whether or not social media is part of the cause. A break from an app does not treat depression or an anxiety disorder.

It also helps to notice whether your use has become problematic. The test from the research is simple. Does it interfere with your daily life? If you keep trying to cut back and cannot, tell a therapist. Our guide on when to seek therapy lists more signs. For condition-specific information, see depression.md and anxiety.md.

If you or a teen in your care has thoughts of suicide, call or text 988 in the US to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Call 911 if there is immediate danger.

The bottom line

Social media can harm mental health, but the evidence does not support a simple verdict. Average links with time online are weak. Links with heavy and problematic use are more consistent. Most of the data cannot prove cause. Trials show a small improvement in depressive symptoms when people cut back, and no clear benefit from quitting completely.

Policy groups have reached the same view. A National Academies committee reviewed the research on adolescents and made recommendations that aim to "maximize the good and minimize the bad." Its focus areas include platform design, digital media literacy, and online harassment. For one person, the practical step is the same: look at what your use displaces and how you feel afterward, then adjust.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many hours of social media a day is too much?

No official limit exists. In one US cohort study of 6,595 adolescents, the adjusted risk of depression and anxiety symptoms was higher only in teens who used social media more than 3 hours a day. That study was observational, so the 3-hour mark is a signal and not a proven threshold.

Will deleting social media cure my depression?

No. A 2025 meta-analysis of 10 randomized trials found that reducing social media use lowered depressive symptoms by a small amount. That is not the same as treating clinical depression. If symptoms last for weeks or affect daily life, see a clinician.

Is a full social media detox better than cutting back?

The trials do not show that. A 2025 meta-analysis of 10 abstinence studies found no significant effect on positive mood, negative mood, or life satisfaction. In a separate meta-analysis, trials that reduced use had a larger effect on depression than trials that required abstinence, although the difference was not statistically significant.

Is social media worse for teens than for adults?

The research cannot answer that yet. Most large studies and official reports focus on adolescents. Adult experiments exist, but one review rated 22 of 23 of them as weak in quality, and most used university students.

Do most teens use social media every day?

Yes. In the CDC's 2023 Youth Risk Behavior Survey, 77.0% of US high school students used social media at least several times a day. The Surgeon General's 2023 advisory reported that up to 95% of youth aged 13 to 17 use a social media platform.
