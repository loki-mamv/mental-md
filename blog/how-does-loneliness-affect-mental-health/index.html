How Does Loneliness Affect Mental Health?

Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Loneliness raises the risk of depression, anxiety, and thoughts of self-harm. The World Health Organization reports that lonely people are twice as likely to get depressed. Long-term studies suggest loneliness often comes first and depression follows. The good news: loneliness responds to treatment, and the most effective approaches target how you think about social situations.

What is loneliness, and how is it different from being alone?

Loneliness is a feeling. The CDC defines it as feeling alone or disconnected from others, without meaningful or close relationships. Social isolation is different. It describes a lack of relationships, contact, and social support that you can count from the outside.

The two often overlap, but not always. The CDC points out that a person with a lot of friends can still feel lonely. Someone who lives alone may feel well connected. What matters for mental health is the gap between the connection you want and the connection you have.

This distinction shapes treatment. If the problem is isolation, more contact can help. If the problem is loneliness inside a busy social life, the fix is usually about the quality of relationships and how you read them.

How common is loneliness?

It is very common. According to the CDC, about 1 in 3 U.S. adults report feeling lonely. About 1 in 4 report not having social and emotional support.

In 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory calling loneliness and isolation a public health crisis. It noted that about one in two American adults reported loneliness even before the COVID-19 pandemic.

The problem is global. The WHO Commission on Social Connection estimated in 2025 that 1 in 6 people worldwide is affected by loneliness. Rates were highest in lower-income countries: about 24% in low-income countries compared with about 11% in high-income countries.

How does loneliness affect depression and anxiety?

Loneliness is one of the clearest social risk factors for depression. The evidence comes from several directions:

  • Pooled data. A 2018 meta-analysis of 88 studies and 40,068 people found that loneliness had a moderate, significant effect on depression.
  • Timing. A 5-year study of adults aged 50 to 68 found that loneliness predicted later increases in depressive symptoms, but not the other way around. The link held after accounting for stress, social support, and personality.
  • Global data. The WHO reports that lonely people are twice as likely to get depressed. They also face higher risk of anxiety and thoughts of self-harm or suicide.

The CDC lists depression, anxiety, suicidality, and self-harm among the risks tied to loneliness and isolation.

Loneliness and depression can also feed each other in daily life. Low mood makes it harder to reach out. Pulling back leads to more loneliness. Many people start to read neutral social cues as rejection, which makes the next step even harder. If low mood has lasted two weeks or more, depression.md covers how depression is recognized and treated.

Does loneliness affect the body too?

Yes. The Surgeon General's advisory concluded that social isolation and loneliness are independent risk factors for cardiovascular disease, dementia, depression, and early death from all causes. It said that lacking connection can raise the risk of premature death to levels comparable to smoking daily.

The strongest single source on death risk is a 2015 meta-analysis of studies from 1980 to 2014. After controlling for other factors, it found:

  • Loneliness was linked to a 26% higher likelihood of death.
  • Social isolation was linked to a 29% higher likelihood.
  • Living alone was linked to a 32% higher likelihood.

The authors found the effect was similar for the feeling of loneliness and for measurable isolation. The link was stronger in groups with an average age under 65.

The CDC also lists heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and dementia among the physical risks. Globally, the WHO estimates loneliness is linked to more than 871,000 deaths each year, about 100 every hour.

Who is most at risk?

Anyone can feel lonely, but some groups carry more risk. The WHO found that 17% to 21% of people aged 13 to 29 reported loneliness, with the highest rates among teenagers. Lonely teens were 22% more likely to get lower grades or qualifications.

Other common risk points include:

  • Major life changes, such as a move, a breakup, retirement, or the death of a partner.
  • Chronic illness, disability, or hearing loss that makes social contact harder.
  • Caregiving, which can crowd out time with friends.
  • Living with a mental health condition such as depression, social anxiety, or PTSD.

People with ongoing mental health conditions often face stigma too. That can make them less likely to share how disconnected they feel.

What actually helps with loneliness?

A meta-analysis of loneliness interventions looked at four strategies: improving social skills, boosting social support, adding chances for social contact, and addressing unhelpful social thinking. In the strongest (randomized) studies, the approaches that addressed unhelpful social thinking worked best.

This fits how loneliness works. A lonely brain tends to expect rejection and scan for threat. Changing those patterns makes new contact feel safer and more rewarding. Cognitive behavioral therapy works on exactly this. Our guide to types of therapy compared explains how CBT and other approaches differ.

Small, regular steps also matter. The WHO suggests reaching out to a friend in need and putting your phone away to be fully present in conversation. Other steps include greeting a neighbor, joining a local group, or volunteering. Shared-experience groups can help too. See what peer support is and how it works.

Consider professional help if loneliness comes with any of these:

  1. Low mood, hopelessness, or loss of interest lasting two weeks or more.
  2. Avoiding people because of fear or intense self-consciousness.
  3. Trouble sleeping, eating, or working.
  4. Thoughts of self-harm or suicide.

Our guide on when to seek therapy covers the signs in more detail. If you are in crisis in the U.S., call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

The bottom line

Loneliness is a common feeling with real mental health effects. It roughly doubles the risk of depression, and long-term data suggest it often comes before depressive symptoms rather than after. It also raises the risk of anxiety, self-harm, heart disease, dementia, and early death. The most effective help targets how you interpret social situations, often through therapy, combined with small, steady steps toward connection.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you feel lonely even if you have friends?

Yes. Loneliness is about the gap between the connection you want and the connection you have. The CDC notes that a person with many friends can still feel lonely. Quality and closeness matter more than the number of contacts.

Is loneliness a mental illness?

No. Loneliness is not a diagnosis on its own. It is a common human feeling. When it lasts, it raises the risk of conditions like depression and anxiety, so persistent loneliness is worth taking seriously.

Does social media make loneliness better or worse?

It depends on how you use it. Passive scrolling rarely replaces real connection. The WHO suggests putting your phone away to be fully present in conversation. Using apps to plan in-person time with people tends to help more than scrolling alone.

How long does it take to feel less lonely?

There is no fixed timeline. Regular, repeated contact with the same people builds closeness over weeks to months. Therapy that targets negative social thinking can speed this up by making contact feel less risky.

When should loneliness be treated as an emergency?

Treat it as urgent if it comes with thoughts of self-harm or suicide. In the U.S., call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.
