Why Is It So Hard to Find a Therapist Who Takes Insurance?

September 8, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Insurance pays therapists less than it pays other clinicians, so many never join a network or leave one. In the American Psychological Association's 2024 survey, 34% of psychologists were not in-network with any insurer. The therapists who do take insurance are often full, and plan directories list many who are unreachable or not accepting new patients. A federal parity law exists, but the 2024 rule meant to fix network gaps is currently not being enforced.

Why do so many therapists refuse insurance?

Because they can. Demand for therapy is high enough that a private-pay practice can stay full without any insurer. The APA 2024 Practitioner Pulse Survey of 853 psychologists found that 34% were not in-network with any public or private plan. Close to 1 in 5 (18%) had never joined an insurance panel in their careers. Psychologists in private practice had the lowest participation in Medicaid.

Psychiatrists have been leaving networks for longer. A JAMA Psychiatry analysis of national survey data found that only 55.3% of psychiatrists accepted private insurance in 2009 to 2010, compared with 88.7% of other physicians. For Medicaid the figures were 43.1% and 73.0%. The gap has not closed. KFF reports that only 36% of psychiatrists accept new Medicaid patients, against 71% of physicians overall.

If the job titles confuse you, our guide to psychiatrist vs psychologist vs therapist explains who does what.

Does insurance pay therapists less than other clinicians?

Yes, and the gap is measurable. RTI International analyzed claims for more than 22 million people across all 50 states from 2019 through 2021. In-network office visit reimbursement was 22% higher for medical and surgical clinicians than for behavioral clinicians on average. At the 75th percentile it was 48% higher, and at the 95th percentile 70% higher. Physician assistants were paid 19% more per office visit than psychiatrists and 23% more than psychologists.

Low pay pushes clinicians out, and patients follow them out of network. The same RTI study found patients went out of network 3.5 times more often for behavioral health clinicians than for medical clinicians. For psychiatrists the multiple was 8.9, and for psychologists 10.6.

Federal auditors heard the same story. In a 2022 GAO report, representatives from 19 of 29 stakeholder organizations said low reimbursement rates contributed to the problem. Some noted that providers can get paid more outside plan networks. Add the paperwork of claims, prior authorization, and audits, and a cash-only practice looks simpler to run.

What is a ghost network?

A ghost network is a provider directory full of names you cannot actually use. The provider may have retired, moved, stopped taking the plan, or closed to new patients. The directory still lists them, so the network looks larger than it is.

The Senate Finance Committee tested this in 2023. Staff called 120 mental health providers listed by 12 Medicare Advantage plans in 6 states. One third (33%) of the listings were wrong numbers, non-working numbers, or unreturned calls. Staff could book an appointment only 18% of the time. More than 80% of the listed providers were "ghosts": unreachable, not accepting new patients, or not in-network. In one case, the earliest appointment offered was 10 months away.

The GAO report above found the same pattern in Medicaid and employer plans. It cited inaccurate or out-of-date network information, and in-network providers who were not accepting new patients or had long waits. So the list your insurer gives you is a starting point, not a promise.

Is there an actual shortage of therapists?

Yes, in most of the country. As of December 31, 2025, HRSA had designated 6,807 mental health professional shortage areas, home to about 137 million people. Those areas have only about 27% of the practitioners they need. Removing every designation would take roughly 6,800 more psychiatrists.

The therapists who exist are busy. In the APA survey, 53% of psychologists had no openings for new patients. Among those with a waitlist, 17% reported waits of four months or longer. Psychologists were contacted by an average of 8.8 new people seeking care each month. A third (32%) said they could not meet the demand from their current patients.

Public programs are hit hardest. A 2024 HHS Office of Inspector General report found that behavioral health providers who actively served Medicare and Medicaid enrollees made up about one third of the total behavioral health workforce in the counties studied. Many enrollees traveled long distances to reach them.

Doesn't the parity law fix this?

Not yet. The Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act of 2008 requires most plans to cover mental health no less favorably than medical care. CMS explains that copays, deductibles, visit limits, and prior authorization rules for mental health cannot be more restrictive than those for medical benefits. The law applies to individual plans and to group plans with more than 50 employees.

Network size and pay rates are harder to police because they are not written as numbers in a benefit summary. Regulators call these "nonquantitative treatment limitations." On September 9, 2024, the Departments of Labor, HHS, and Treasury issued a final rule that named network composition and reimbursement methods as limitations plans must analyze. Plans would have to collect data on out-of-network use, and a material gap in access would count as a strong indicator of a violation.

Then enforcement stopped. The ERISA Industry Committee sued in January 2025. On May 15, 2025, the Departments announced they would not enforce the new parts of the 2024 rule until the litigation ends, plus 18 months, while they reconsider it. The underlying statute still applies. The tool built to fix ghost networks is on hold.

How do I actually find a therapist who takes my plan?

The gap is real, but people do get through it. These steps shorten the search:

  1. Call your insurer and ask for a list of behavioral health providers accepting new patients, not the full directory. Ask for the date the list was verified.
  2. Call each office and ask two questions: "Are you in-network with my exact plan?" and "When is your next new-patient opening?"
  3. Widen the search to licensed clinical social workers, licensed professional counselors, and marriage and family therapists. They outnumber psychologists and are more likely to be in-network.
  4. Consider video visits. Licensed clinicians anywhere in your state count as in-network if they hold a contract with your plan. Our review of online versus in-person therapy covers what the evidence shows.
  5. Ask for a network adequacy exception. If no in-network therapist can see you within a reasonable time, many plans and state laws let you see an out-of-network therapist at in-network cost. Get the denial in writing if they refuse.
  6. Check whether your plan has out-of-network benefits. Superbills and a deductible may still beat waiting months.

If you end up paying cash, our breakdown of therapy costs without insurance lists typical price ranges and lower-cost options. And if anxiety is the reason you are looking, anxiety.md covers treatment options while you search.

The stakes are high. In KFF polling, 43% of insured adults who rated their mental health as fair or poor said they needed care in the past year and did not get it. Of those, 44% could not afford it, 37% said insurance would not cover it, and 44% could not find a provider they trusted.

The bottom line

Finding a therapist who takes insurance is hard because insurers pay behavioral clinicians about 22% less than medical clinicians for the same visit, so a third of psychologists and nearly half of psychiatrists stay out of networks. Directories overstate who is available, with more than 80% of listed providers unreachable or unavailable in a Senate test. Shortage areas cover about 137 million people. The 2024 parity rule that targeted network gaps is not being enforced. Your best moves are to call, widen the provider types, use video visits, and demand a network exception when the plan cannot deliver.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get reimbursed if I see an out-of-network therapist?

Only if your plan includes out-of-network benefits, which many HMO plans do not. PPO plans usually pay a share after you meet a separate out-of-network deductible. Ask the therapist for a superbill and submit it to your insurer for each visit.

What is a network adequacy exception?

It is a request to your plan to cover an out-of-network therapist at in-network rates because no in-network therapist is available within a reasonable time or distance. Document every call you made and every wait time you were quoted. If the plan refuses, you can appeal and file a complaint with your state insurance department.

Why does my insurer's directory list therapists who don't take my plan?

Directories are updated by plans and providers, and both fall behind. Clinicians leave panels, move, retire, or close to new patients without the listing changing. Regulators call these outdated listings ghost networks, and audits show they are common.

Are counselors and social workers a good substitute for a psychologist?

For most talk therapy, yes. Licensed clinical social workers and licensed professional counselors provide evidence-based therapies such as CBT. Psychologists add formal testing and assessment, which most people seeking therapy do not need.

Will Medicaid or Medicare cover a therapist?

Both cover outpatient mental health care, but fewer clinicians accept these programs. KFF reports that only 36% of psychiatrists take new Medicaid patients. Community mental health centers and federally qualified health centers are the most reliable route for public coverage.
