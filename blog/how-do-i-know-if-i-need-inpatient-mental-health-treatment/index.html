How Do I Know if I Need Inpatient Mental Health Treatment?

The short answer: You may need inpatient mental health treatment if you are at immediate risk of harming yourself or someone else, cannot keep yourself safe or fed, or have lost touch with reality. It is also an option when less intensive care has not worked. If you are in crisis now, call or text 988, or call 911 in a life-threatening emergency. A clinician, usually in an emergency room, makes the final call on admission.

What signs point to inpatient psychiatric care?

Inpatient care is the most intensive level of mental health treatment. You stay in a locked or secured hospital unit with staff present 24 hours a day. Federal Medicare rules describe it as care "of an intensity that can be provided appropriately only in an inpatient hospital setting" (42 CFR 412.27).

Clinicians tend to look for one or more of these signs:

  • Immediate risk to yourself: a suicide plan, recent attempt, or strong urges you cannot resist.
  • Immediate risk to others: serious threats or a plan to hurt someone.
  • Inability to care for yourself: not eating, drinking, sleeping, or taking critical medicine because of a mental health condition.
  • Psychosis or severe mania: hallucinations, delusions, or behavior that puts you in danger.
  • Failed lower levels of care: symptoms keep getting worse despite therapy, medicine, or a day program.
  • A need for close medical watch: for example, during a major medicine change or a complex diagnosis.

Feeling very bad is not, on its own, a reason for a hospital stay. Many people with severe depression or anxiety do well with outpatient care. The question is safety and whether you can be treated well outside a hospital.

Inpatient care also differs from day programs in where you sleep. In inpatient care, you stay overnight on the unit. In day programs, you go home each evening.

An intensive outpatient program (IOP) gives you several hours of treatment a few days a week. A partial hospitalization program (PHP) is a full-day program, often five days a week. Both can be a step down after a hospital stay, or a way to avoid one. If you can stay safe at home overnight, one of these may fit better than admission.

How do you get admitted to a psychiatric hospital?

Most admissions start in one of three ways.

  1. The emergency room. This is the most common route. Under the federal EMTALA rule, a hospital ER must give anyone who comes in a medical screening exam, regardless of ability to pay. The rule's definition of an emergency includes "psychiatric disturbances" (42 CFR 489.24).
  2. Crisis services. SAMHSA's crisis care guidance builds on three parts: someone to contact (such as 988), someone to respond (such as mobile crisis teams), and a safe place for help (SAMHSA). A crisis team can help decide if you need a hospital or a short-term crisis center.
  3. Direct referral. A psychiatrist or therapist who knows you may arrange admission. This is less common and depends on bed space.

In the ER, staff check your physical health first. Then a mental health clinician assesses you. If you are admitted, a full psychiatric evaluation follows on the unit. Medicare rules require that evaluation within 60 hours of admission (42 CFR 412.27).

When should you call 988 instead of going to the ER?

If you are thinking about suicide or are in emotional distress, call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org. The Lifeline is free, confidential, and open 24 hours a day. A trained counselor will ask if you are safe, then listen and share resources (988 Lifeline). Veterans can call 988 and press 1.

The National Institute of Mental Health says to call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room in life-threatening situations. Use the ER if you have already hurt yourself, taken an overdose, or cannot stay safe right now. A 988 counselor can also help you work out if you need hospital care.

Is inpatient admission voluntary?

Most people who go into a psychiatric hospital agree to it. Voluntary admission means you sign in yourself. You keep more control, but many states require you to give written notice before you leave, and staff may hold you for a short time while they reassess.

Involuntary admission is different. Every state lets clinicians hold a person who is a danger to self or others, or who cannot care for themselves, even without consent. The rules, time limits, and court steps vary by state. If you worry about losing a say in your care, a psychiatric advance directive lets you record your choices ahead of time.

What happens during an inpatient stay, and how long is it?

Each patient gets a written treatment plan with a diagnosis, short- and long-term goals, and the treatments to be used (42 CFR 412.27). A typical day includes:

  • Meetings with a psychiatrist, often daily, to adjust medicine
  • Group therapy and skills groups
  • Check-ins with nurses and social workers
  • Structured meals, rest, and set visiting hours

Most stays are short. In a 2016 analysis of U.S. community hospitals, stays for a principal mental or substance use diagnosis averaged 6.4 days (AHRQ HCUP). Depressive disorders were the most common reason, at 26.1 percent of these stays. Schizophrenia stays ran longer, at 11 days on average, and eating disorder stays were the longest.

The goal is stabilization, not a full recovery. The hospital aims to get you safe enough to continue treatment at a lower level of care.

Does insurance cover inpatient mental health care?

Most health plans cover it when it is medically necessary. Medicare Part A covers inpatient mental health care in a general or psychiatric hospital. In 2026, you pay a $1,736 deductible per benefit period, then $0 a day for days 1 to 60 and $434 a day for days 61 to 90 (Medicare.gov). Part B covers doctor services in the hospital, and you usually pay 20 percent of the approved amount.

One limit surprises people. In a freestanding psychiatric hospital, Part A pays for only 190 days in your lifetime. That limit does not apply to a psychiatric unit inside a general hospital. Medicare also requires a physician to certify that you need inpatient care, then recertify by day 12 and at least every 30 days after (42 CFR 424.14).

What happens when you leave the hospital?

Discharge planning starts early. Before you leave, the team should set up follow-up care, such as a therapist appointment, a psychiatrist visit, or a step-down IOP or PHP. Your record must include a discharge summary with recommendations for aftercare (42 CFR 412.27).

The weeks after discharge are a high-risk time. A meta-analysis of 100 studies found that suicide rates were highest in the first 3 months after leaving a psychiatric facility (Chung et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2017). Rates were also highest among people admitted for suicidal thoughts or behavior. That is why a follow-up appointment within days, a written safety plan, and support from family matter so much.

If depression drove your admission, depression.md has more on treatment options you may continue after discharge.

The bottom line

Inpatient care is for safety: when you are at immediate risk, cannot care for yourself, or are not improving with less intensive treatment. You do not have to make the decision alone. Call or text 988 to talk it through, or go to an ER if you are in danger now. Most stays last about a week, and the real work continues after you leave.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I check myself into a psychiatric hospital?

Yes. Many people go to an emergency room and say they need help to stay safe. A clinician assesses you and decides if inpatient care is the right level. Bed space and insurance can affect where you go.

Can I leave a psychiatric hospital whenever I want?

If you signed in voluntarily, you can usually ask to leave. Some states require written notice first, and staff may hold you briefly to reassess your safety. If they believe you are a danger to yourself or others, they may start an involuntary hold under state law.

Can I keep my phone during an inpatient stay?

Rules vary by hospital. Many units limit or hold personal phones and items that could be used for self-harm, and offer a shared phone at set times. Ask the unit about its policy when you arrive.

Will my employer find out about a psychiatric hospital stay?

Your health records are private, and the hospital cannot share them with your employer without your permission. You may need a doctor's note for time off, which does not have to state a diagnosis. You may also have rights to job-protected leave depending on your employer.

What if I cannot afford inpatient treatment?

Hospital emergency rooms must screen anyone who comes in, regardless of ability to pay. Medicaid, Medicare, and most private plans cover medically necessary inpatient mental health care. Ask to speak with a hospital social worker or financial counselor about coverage and payment help.
