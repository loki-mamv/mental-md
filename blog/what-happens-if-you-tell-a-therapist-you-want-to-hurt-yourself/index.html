What Happens If You Tell a Therapist You Want to Hurt Yourself?

The short answer: Most of the time, telling a therapist you want to hurt yourself leads to a careful conversation, not a hospital. Your therapist will ask questions to understand your risk, build a safety plan with you, and may want to see you more often for a while. Confidentiality has limits: if you are in serious and imminent danger, a therapist may contact others or seek emergency care, and the rules vary by state. If you are in crisis right now, call or text the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline at 988.

What usually happens right after you say it?

Your therapist will not end the session or call for help the moment you speak. The usual first step is a set of calm, direct questions. The goal is to understand how serious the thoughts are and what is driving them.

Expect questions like these:

  • How often do the thoughts come, and how long do they last?
  • Are they new, or have they grown stronger recently?
  • Do you want to die, or do you want the pain to stop?
  • Have you thought about how or when you would act?
  • What has kept you safe so far?

These questions track the warning signs the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) lists. They include talking about wanting to die, feeling hopeless or trapped, and "making a plan or researching ways to die." NIMH urges people to get help as soon as possible, "particularly if the behavior is new or has increased recently."

If "hurt yourself" means self-harm without wanting to die, say so. Your therapist still needs to know, and the same careful questions apply. The answers shape what happens next.

How does a safety plan work?

For most people who are not in immediate danger, the next step is a written safety plan. The best-known version is the Safety Planning Intervention, developed by Stanley and Brown in 2012. According to a Defense Health Agency evidence brief, the therapist and patient build the plan together. It covers:

  1. Your personal warning signs that a crisis is building.
  2. Coping strategies you can use on your own.
  3. People and places that offer support or distraction.
  4. Contact information for professional help, including 988.
  5. Steps to make your surroundings safer.

The evidence is encouraging. A 2018 study in JAMA Psychiatry followed 1,640 patients seen in nine Veterans Health Administration emergency departments. Patients who got a safety plan plus at least two follow-up phone calls had 45% fewer suicidal behaviors over six months than those who got usual care (3.03% vs 5.29%). They also had more than double the odds of attending an outpatient mental health visit. This was a cohort comparison, not a randomized trial.

Your therapist may also suggest more frequent sessions or brief check-ins until things settle. That is added support, not a punishment.

Does it matter whether the thoughts are passive or you have a plan?

Yes, it changes the response. Clinicians often separate passive thoughts, such as wishing you were dead, from active thoughts about ending your life. A specific plan with intent to act soon is the situation that calls for urgent action.

Passive thoughts are still worth reporting. A 2020 meta-analysis of 86 studies in Psychological Medicine found passive ideation in 5.8% of the general population over one year and 10.6% over a lifetime. It was strongly linked to suicide attempts. Its correlates were largely similar to those of active ideation.

In practice, passive thoughts usually lead to closer monitoring and a safety plan. A clear plan, intent and a timeline lead to faster steps to keep you safe.

When can a therapist break confidentiality?

Therapy is confidential, but not without limits. The American Psychological Association ethics code requires psychologists to discuss those limits "at the outset of the relationship." It lets them disclose information without consent only as required by law, or where the law permits it for purposes such as protecting "the client/patient, psychologist, or others from harm." If no one explained this to you, ask. It is a good topic for your first therapy session.

Federal privacy law sets a high bar. Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule, 45 CFR 164.512(j), a provider may disclose information when they believe in good faith that it is necessary "to prevent or lessen a serious and imminent threat." The disclosure must go to someone "reasonably able to prevent or lessen the threat," such as a family member or emergency services. The rule permits this. It does not require it.

State law adds another layer. The National Conference of State Legislatures tracks state "duty to warn" laws. Some states make disclosure mandatory, others permit it, and a few set no duty. Most of these laws focus on threats to other people. A few, such as New York's, also cover danger to the patient.

Will you be sent to a hospital?

Usually not. Hospital care is generally reserved for people at imminent risk who cannot stay safe with outpatient support. Even then, a therapist will often talk with you first about going voluntarily.

Involuntary care is the exception. A SAMHSA report on civil commitment notes that "the vast majority of mental health services today are provided on a voluntary basis." Commitment standards "vary from state to state." Most states require the least restrictive setting that meets a person's needs.

If an emergency hold does happen, it has limits. The same report found that evaluation holds range from 23 hours in North Dakota to 10 days in New Hampshire and Rhode Island. The most common period is 72 hours, used in at least 17 states. During a hold, people are typically offered treatment and the chance to accept a voluntary admission.

Why is it worth telling your therapist?

Many people fear that saying the words will make things worse. The research says otherwise. A 2014 review in Psychological Medicine found no study showing a significant increase in suicidal thoughts after people were asked about them. The authors concluded that talking about suicide "may in fact reduce, rather than increase suicidal ideation."

Your therapist can only help with what they know. Hiding these thoughts means your treatment plan misses the most urgent problem. Shame keeps many people quiet, and mental health stigma makes it harder. Therapists hear about these thoughts often and are trained to respond calmly.

If depression is behind the thoughts, depression.md explains symptoms and treatment options in plain language.

The bottom line

Telling a therapist you want to hurt yourself usually leads to questions, a safety plan and closer support. Confidentiality ends mainly when there is a serious and imminent threat, and the details depend on your state. Passive thoughts and a specific plan get different responses, but both deserve to be said out loud. If you are in crisis now, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, which is free, confidential and available 24/7. If you are not yet in therapy, see when to seek therapy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will my therapist tell my family I have suicidal thoughts?

Usually not without your consent. HIPAA allows a provider to share information when they believe it is necessary to prevent a serious and imminent threat, and only with someone able to reduce that threat. Many therapists will ask if you want to involve a trusted person in your safety plan. If you are under 18, ask your therapist how the rules apply to you.

Does talking about suicide put the idea in my head?

No. A 2014 review in Psychological Medicine found no study showing a significant rise in suicidal thoughts after people were asked about them. The authors wrote that talking about suicide may reduce suicidal thoughts. Asking is a normal part of good care.

What if I am not sure whether I would act on the thoughts?

Say exactly that. Uncertainty is common, and your therapist can work with it. Research shows even passive thoughts are linked to suicide attempts, so it is worth raising them early, before they grow.

Is calling 988 the same as calling 911?

No. SAMHSA says most people who contact 988 are helped by the counselor during the call, text or chat without involving 911. A counselor contacts 911 only when there is an imminent risk to someone's life that cannot be reduced during the contact. The service is free and available 24/7.

Can I ask about confidentiality before I share anything?

Yes, and it is a smart first step. The APA ethics code says psychologists should explain the limits of confidentiality at the start of the relationship and again when new circumstances come up. Knowing the rules ahead of time can make it easier to speak openly.
