How Do I Support a Partner With Depression Without Burning Out?

September 21, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Support your partner with steady, practical help and good listening, but do not become their only source of care. Research on spouses of people with depression finds real strain: worry, extra tasks, and more anxiety and low mood of their own. Set clear limits, bring in a clinician and other people, and look after your own health. If your partner talks about suicide or shows warning signs, call or text 988.

Why does a partner's depression wear you down?

Depression changes the whole household, not only the person who has it. A study in the Journal of Affective Disorders surveyed 260 spouses and relatives of people with depression. About 25% to 50% worried about the patient's health, treatment, safety, and future. Many had to urge the person to do things or took over their tasks.

The same study found relationship strain and a sense of burden, especially during acute episodes. Caregivers often felt distressed enough to see a health practitioner themselves. Children in the home were affected too, with more sleep problems and difficult behavior.

A large Norwegian population study, the Nord-Trondelag (HUNT) study, looked at 9,740 couples. Spouses of people with a mental disorder reported lower well-being and more symptoms of anxiety and depression than other spouses. The differences were moderate, and most spouses did not reach a clinical level. That is useful to know: strain is common, but it is not inevitable collapse.

A 2026 meta-analysis pooled 11 studies with 1,366 informal caregivers of people with depression. Burden approached a moderate level on average. It was higher when depression was severe and when the illness had lasted more than a year.

What kind of support actually helps?

The National Institute of Mental Health gives a short list for helping a loved one. It starts with helping them see a health care provider or mental health professional. It then suggests:

  • Offering support, understanding, patience, and encouragement.
  • Inviting them out for walks, outings, and other activities.
  • Helping with medication reminders and getting to appointments.
  • Reminding them that, with time and treatment, depression can lift.

Listening matters more than fixing. You do not need to argue your partner out of their feelings. Reflect what you hear, ask what would help today, and keep offers small and specific. "I can call the clinic at lunch" lands better than "let me know if you need anything."

Be patient with treatment timing. NIMH notes that antidepressants usually take 4 to 8 weeks to work, and finding the right treatment can take trial and error. How do you set boundaries without abandoning your partner?

A boundary is a limit on what you do, not a punishment. The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) says that if helping someone feels like it is taking over your life, you need to tell them. It suggests two practical moves:

  1. Set times. If your loved one reaches out constantly, agree on set times to talk, and keep to them.
  2. Say what you will and will not do. Name the tasks you can take on and the ones you cannot.

SAMHSA also suggests helping the person think of other people they can count on. This is the key to avoiding burnout. You should be one support among several, not the only one. A therapist, a prescriber, friends, family, and peer groups can all carry part of the load.

Some boundaries protect your relationship too. You might keep one evening a week that is not about the illness. You might decide that finances or parenting decisions wait for a calmer day. Boundaries work best when you explain them with warmth and apply them consistently.

If your partner will not engage with care at all, that is a different problem. Our guide on how to help someone who refuses mental health treatment covers that situation.

Does couples therapy help with depression?

It can, with caveats. A 2018 Cochrane review included 14 trials with 651 participants. Couple therapy aims to change negative interaction patterns and build mutual support in the relationship.

  • Versus individual therapy: no clear difference in depressive symptoms at the end of treatment or at follow-up of 6 months or more.
  • Relationship distress: couple therapy appeared better than individual therapy at reducing distress, with a larger effect in couples who were already distressed.
  • Versus no or minimal treatment: a large benefit, but from only three small studies.

The reviewers rated the evidence as low or very low quality, so firm conclusions are not possible. Most participants had moderate depression, so results may not apply to severe depression. In practice, couples therapy is a reasonable option when the relationship itself is under strain. It works alongside, not instead of, your partner's own treatment.

When is it a crisis?

Some signs mean you should act now rather than wait. The NIMH warning signs of suicide include:

  • Talking about wanting to die, great guilt or shame, or being a burden to others.
  • Feeling empty, hopeless, trapped, or having no reason to live.
  • Making a plan or researching ways to die.
  • Withdrawing, saying goodbye, or giving away important items.
  • Taking dangerous risks, or using drugs or alcohol more often.

NIMH advises getting help as soon as possible, particularly if the behavior is new or has increased. Call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org. The 988 Lifeline says you can contact it about someone else, and that you should never keep a friend's plan to hurt themselves a secret. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.

How do you take care of yourself while you support them?

Your own health is part of the care plan. The American Psychological Association's 2026 resolution on family caregivers estimates that 63 million Americans, or 24% of the population, were family caregivers in 2025. It supports routine screening of caregivers for burden, stress, and depressive symptoms.

Practical steps that follow from the research:

  • Protect the basics. Keep regular sleep, meals, movement, and time outside. SAMHSA suggests making some time for yourself each day.
  • Get your own support. NAMI Family-to-Family is a free, 8-session class for family members, partners, and friends. NAMI calls it evidence-based. The NAMI HelpLine is 800-950-6264.
  • Watch for your own symptoms. If you notice lasting low mood, poor sleep, or dread, see a clinician for yourself. The 988 Lifeline says supporters can reach out for their own support too.

The bottom line

Loving someone with depression is hard work, and research confirms the strain is real. The most helpful partners listen, offer concrete help, and encourage treatment. They also set limits, share the load with clinicians and other people, and look after their own health. Couples therapy is an option when the relationship is strained, though the evidence is limited. Know the warning signs, and call or text 988 if you are worried about suicide.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal to feel resentful toward a partner with depression?

Yes. Studies of spouses of people with depression describe relationship strain, extra tasks, and a sense of burden. Resentment is a signal that the load is too heavy or too one-sided, not a sign that you are a bad partner. Talking to a therapist or a family support group can help you sort through it.

Should I tell my partner's doctor what I am seeing at home?

You can share what you observe, such as sleep changes or talk of hopelessness, with your partner's permission where possible. Privacy rules may limit what the clinician tells you back, but they can still listen. If you are worried about safety, call or text 988 for guidance.

Can I get depressed from living with a depressed partner?

Research shows spouses of people with a mental disorder report more anxiety and depressive symptoms on average, though most do not reach a clinical level. Lasting low mood, poor sleep, or loss of interest in your own life are reasons to see a clinician for yourself. Caring for your own health also helps you stay steady for your partner.

What should I say when my partner says they feel like a burden?

Take it seriously, because talking about being a burden is one of the NIMH warning signs of suicide. The 988 Lifeline lists asking directly as the first step, so ask whether they are thinking about suicide. If the answer is yes or you are unsure, call or text 988 together.

Is it okay to leave a relationship with someone who has depression?

That is a personal decision, and no article can make it for you. Many couples find that treatment, boundaries, and couples therapy ease the strain. If you are considering leaving, a therapist of your own can help you think it through, and a safety plan matters if your partner is at risk.
