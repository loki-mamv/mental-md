What Is Peer Support in Mental Health, and Does It Work?

September 23, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Peer support is help from a trained person who has lived through a mental health or substance use condition and is now in recovery. It comes in many forms, from one-on-one work with a certified peer specialist to support groups, warmlines, and peer-run respite houses. The research is honest but modest: reviews find small gains in recovery, hope, and empowerment, while effects on symptoms and hospital use are mixed and often low-certainty. It works best alongside clinical care, not in place of it.

What is a peer support specialist?

The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) describes peer support workers as people who have been successful in the recovery process and who help others in similar situations. Their tool is shared experience, not a clinical license. SAMHSA lists what they do:

  • Advocate for people in recovery.
  • Share resources and build skills.
  • Build community and relationships.
  • Lead recovery groups.
  • Mentor and help set goals.

A peer specialist does not diagnose, prescribe, or deliver psychotherapy. The value is different. Someone who has sat in a psychiatric waiting room, or rebuilt a life after a hospital stay, can say "this is survivable" with a kind of credibility a clinician cannot.

Certification is set state by state. In its 2007 letter to state Medicaid directors, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) said peer providers must complete training and certification as defined by the state. It also required ongoing continuing education, like other provider types.

Where do you find peer support?

Peer support shows up in more places than most people expect. Common settings include:

  • Clinics and treatment teams. Certified peer specialists work in community mental health centers, hospitals, and programs such as a partial hospitalization program.
  • Support groups. NAMI support groups are peer-led and free. NAMI Connection is for people with mental health conditions, and NAMI Family Support Group is for relatives and friends. Many meet online.
  • Warmlines. A 2024 SAMHSA report on peer support in crisis care describes warmlines as noncrisis lines for emotional support. They are staffed by people with lived experience and have protocols to escalate when a caller needs more help.
  • Peer-run respites. The same report describes these as voluntary, short-term, home-like programs for people in distress who do not need inpatient care. They are run by and for people with lived experience.
  • Clubhouses. These are member-run community centers focused on work, education, and social connection for people with serious mental illness. A 2018 systematic review of 52 studies called the model promising, with trial support for employment, fewer hospitalizations, and quality of life, but asked for more rigorous research.

Does peer support work?

The best evidence says it helps some outcomes a little, and the certainty is limited. Here are the main reviews.

A 2014 meta-analysis in BMC Psychiatry pooled 18 randomized trials with 5,597 people with severe mental illness. It found little or no evidence of effects on hospitalization, overall symptoms, or satisfaction with services. It did find some evidence of gains in hope, recovery, and empowerment, though these were not consistent across trials.

A larger 2023 meta-analysis in Psychological Medicine pooled 28 trials with 4,152 people. Peer support produced small but significant gains in clinical recovery (effect size g = 0.19) and personal recovery (g = 0.15). It did not improve functional recovery, such as work or daily living. The authors urged caution because study quality was modest.

A 2024 umbrella review in BMC Medicine looked at 35 reviews covering 426 studies. Results were mixed. There was some evidence that peer support may improve depression symptoms (especially perinatal depression), self-efficacy, and recovery. Nearly all of the reviews (97%) were rated low or critically low quality.

Cochrane reviews are the most conservative, and they are cautious here. A 2013 Cochrane review covered 11 trials with 2,796 people. It found that teams using peer providers ("consumer-providers") got outcomes no better or worse than teams using professionals in similar roles. There was low-quality evidence of a small drop in crisis and emergency service use, and no evidence of harm.

A 2019 Cochrane review of peer support for schizophrenia and other serious mental illness included 13 trials with 2,479 people. Only two trials gave usable data on key outcomes. The authors rated the evidence very low quality and said very limited data exist.

A fair reading: peer support appears safe, and peers can do some jobs as well as professionals. But strong proof that it prevents hospital stays is still missing.

Can peer support keep people out of the hospital?

Some individual studies are promising. In the CORE trial in England, 441 people leaving crisis home treatment were randomized. Those offered up to 10 sessions with a peer worker were readmitted less often within a year: 29% versus 38%. The authors noted wide confidence intervals and lower admission rates than expected.

For peer respites, a 2015 study in Psychiatric Services matched 139 respite users with 139 similar nonusers. The odds of any inpatient or emergency service use were about 70% lower among respite users. This was an observational study, not a randomized trial, so it cannot prove cause and effect.

Who pays for peer support?

Medicaid is the main payer in the United States. The CMS guidance letter (SMDL #07-011, August 15, 2007) told states they could cover peer support services. It set three core conditions:

  1. Supervision by a competent mental health professional, as defined by the state.
  2. Care coordination within an individualized, person-centered plan of care.
  3. Training and certification as defined by the state, plus continuing education.

Private insurance coverage varies widely. Support groups such as NAMI's are free. If cost or coverage is your barrier to care in general, our guide on why it is hard to find a therapist who takes insurance explains the network problem.

Who is peer support right for, and what is it not?

Peer support may suit you if you feel isolated, distrust the system, or want someone who has been there. It can help with goal setting, navigating services, and staying engaged in treatment. People with depression, bipolar disorder, or PTSD often find condition-specific groups; bipolar.md covers that condition in depth.

It is not a substitute for therapy or medication when those are needed. See our guide to types of therapy compared for clinical options. It is also not emergency care. A warmline is for noncrisis support. If you or someone you know is thinking about suicide, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, available 24/7.

The bottom line

Peer support means help from someone with lived experience of mental illness or addiction, trained and often state-certified. It is widely available through clinics, groups, warmlines, and peer respites, and Medicaid has allowed states to cover it since 2007. The evidence shows small, fairly consistent gains in recovery and hope, with mixed and low-certainty results for symptoms and hospital use. Use it as one layer of support, alongside clinical care.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a peer support specialist the same as a therapist?

No. A peer specialist draws on lived experience of recovery and state training, not a clinical license. They do not diagnose, prescribe, or provide psychotherapy. Many work on the same team as therapists and psychiatrists.

How do you become a certified peer specialist?

Each state sets its own training and certification rules. Federal Medicaid guidance from 2007 says peers must be self-identified people in recovery, complete state-defined training and certification, and keep up continuing education. Check your state behavioral health agency for the specific program.

What is the difference between a warmline and a crisis line?

A warmline offers noncrisis emotional support, usually from people with lived experience. A crisis line such as 988 is for urgent situations, including thoughts of suicide. Warmlines have protocols to connect callers to crisis services when needed.

Are peer support groups free?

Many are. NAMI Connection and NAMI Family Support Group are free and peer-led, and many meet online. Peer specialist services in clinics are often billed to Medicaid, while private insurance coverage varies.

Can family members get peer support too?

Yes. Family peer support is its own field, built on the shared experience of caring for a loved one. NAMI Family Support Group is one example, and some states certify family peer specialists.
