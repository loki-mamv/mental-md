Psychiatrist, Psychologist, or Therapist: What Is the Difference?

August 22, 2026 5 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team

The short answer: a psychiatrist is a medical doctor who diagnoses and prescribes. A psychologist holds a doctorate and provides psychotherapy and psychological testing, but cannot prescribe except in nine jurisdictions. "Therapist" is an umbrella term for licensed master's-level clinicians (LCSW, LPC, LMFT, LMHC) who provide talk therapy. If you want medication, you need a prescriber. If you want therapy, a master's-level therapist is fully qualified to give it.

The titles are confusing partly because they overlap, and partly because insurance directories list them all together with no explanation. Here is the map.

Psychiatrist (MD or DO)

Training: four years of medical school followed by a four-year psychiatry residency, then licensure by a state medical board and usually board certification. Some add a fellowship in child and adolescent, geriatric, addiction, or forensic psychiatry.

What they do: diagnose mental health conditions, prescribe and manage medication, order and interpret medical tests, and rule out physical causes of psychiatric symptoms. Thyroid disease, anemia, sleep apnea, and medication side effects all imitate psychiatric conditions, and this is where medical training earns its keep.

What they usually do not do: weekly psychotherapy. Some psychiatrists still practice therapy, but most modern practice separates the roles: the psychiatrist manages medication in shorter visits every one to three months, and a therapist handles the ongoing work.

When you need one: complex or treatment-resistant conditions, bipolar disorder, psychosis, several medications interacting, or a diagnosis that is genuinely unclear.

Psychologist (PhD or PsyD)

Training: a doctoral degree in psychology, typically four to seven years, plus one to two years of supervised clinical experience and a state licensing exam.

What they do: diagnose, provide individual and group psychotherapy, and, distinctively, administer formal psychological and neuropsychological testing. If you need a structured evaluation for ADHD, a learning disorder, cognitive change, or personality assessment, this is the provider who does it.

Prescribing: generally no. The exception is real but narrow. Prescriptive authority for psychologists has passed into law in nine jurisdictions: New Mexico (2002), Louisiana (2004), Illinois (2014), Iowa (2016), Idaho (2017), Colorado (2023), Utah (2024), Vermont, and Hawaii. Psychologists must complete a master's degree in clinical psychopharmacology and pass a national exam to qualify, and only a few hundred are actively prescribing nationwide (APA Services, RxP chronology). Specially trained psychologists can also prescribe within the Department of Defense, the Public Health Service, and the Indian Health Service.

"Therapist": the umbrella term

This is where most people actually end up, and where the alphabet soup lives. All of these are state-licensed master's-level clinicians who can diagnose and treat mental health conditions with psychotherapy. None can prescribe.

  • LCSW (Licensed Clinical Social Worker): a master's in social work plus supervised clinical hours. Trained in therapy and also in case management, benefits, and systems navigation, which matters more than people expect.
  • LPC / LPCC / LMHC (Licensed Professional Counselor, Licensed Professional Clinical Counselor, Licensed Mental Health Counselor): a master's in counseling or psychology plus supervised hours. The title varies by state for essentially the same scope.
  • LMFT (Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist): a master's in marriage and family therapy plus supervised hours, commonly around 3,000. Trained to treat the relationship or family system, not only the individual.

For weekly therapy, the license letters matter far less than two other things: whether the clinician is trained in the specific method your condition calls for, and whether you can work with them. Exposure and response prevention for OCD, prolonged exposure for PTSD, and CBT-I for insomnia are all specific skills, and plenty of generalist therapists do not have them. Ask directly. Our guide to types of therapy compared covers which method fits which condition.

The prescriber most people forget

Psychiatric mental health nurse practitioners (PMHNPs) prescribe psychiatric medication, and in much of the country they are the prescriber you will actually be able to see.

Between 2011 and 2019, the number of PMHNPs treating Medicare beneficiaries grew 162%, while the number of psychiatrists doing so fell 6%. By 2019, PMHNPs provided nearly one in three mental health prescriber visits to Medicare patients nationally (Cai, Barnett et al., Health Affairs, 2022). The same study found psychiatrists and PMHNPs treated broadly comparable patients with similar services and prescriptions.

Your primary care physician is also a legitimate starting point. Primary care prescribes a large share of all antidepressants in the United States, and for a first episode of uncomplicated depression or anxiety that is an entirely reasonable place to begin.

So who should you book first?

A practical decision rule:

  • Not sure what is going on: a therapist or your primary care physician. Both can assess and point you onward, and both are far easier to get in to see.
  • You want to try medication: primary care physician, psychiatrist, or PMHNP.
  • You want talk therapy: any licensed therapist trained in the relevant method.
  • You need a formal evaluation for ADHD, a learning disorder, or cognitive change: a psychologist.
  • Symptoms are severe, complex, or previous medications have failed: a psychiatrist.
  • You are in crisis: call or text 988. See what actually happens when you call 988.

One caution about sequencing. Psychiatrists have the longest waits of any provider in this list, often months. Booking one "just to start" frequently delays care that a therapist could have begun in two weeks.

What about cost and insurance?

Cost tracks the credential more than the outcome. Psychiatrists generally charge the most per visit, psychologists next, master's-level therapists least, though psychiatry visits are shorter and less frequent so the annual totals can converge. Master's-level clinicians are also the most likely to be in network. Our breakdown of therapy costs without insurance has the numbers.

The bottom line

Prescribing is the dividing line that matters. Psychiatrists, PMHNPs, and primary care physicians prescribe; psychologists do so only in nine jurisdictions with extra training; master's-level therapists never do. For therapy itself, a well-trained LCSW, LPC, or LMFT is not a downgrade from a doctorate. Pick the provider whose tool matches your problem, and do not let the pursuit of the most credentialed option delay starting. Frequently Asked Questions

Who can prescribe psychiatric medication?

Psychiatrists (MD or DO), psychiatric mental health nurse practitioners (PMHNPs), physician associates working under physician supervision, and primary care physicians can all prescribe. Psychologists cannot, with a narrow exception: nine jurisdictions grant prescriptive authority to psychologists who complete a master's degree in clinical psychopharmacology and pass a national exam. Master's-level therapists such as LCSWs, LPCs, and LMFTs cannot prescribe anywhere.

What is the actual difference between a psychologist and a psychiatrist?

Training and tools. A psychiatrist attends medical school and completes a four-year psychiatry residency, and in current practice usually focuses on diagnosis and medication management. A psychologist completes a doctorate (PhD or PsyD) plus supervised clinical hours and a licensing exam, and delivers psychotherapy and formal psychological testing. Neither is senior to the other; they do different jobs.

Is a therapist less qualified than a psychologist?

Not for psychotherapy. "Therapist" is an umbrella term covering licensed master's-level clinicians such as LCSWs, LPCs, LPCCs, LMHCs, and LMFTs, all of whom are state-licensed and trained to diagnose and treat mental health conditions with talk therapy. For weekly therapy, the therapist's training in the specific method usually matters more than the letters after their name. Formal psychological or neuropsychological testing is where a doctoral-level psychologist is required.

Who should I see first if I do not know what I need?

For most people it is either a therapist or their primary care physician. A therapist can assess, diagnose, and start treatment, and can tell you if medication is worth discussing. A primary care physician can prescribe first-line antidepressants and refer onward. Booking a psychiatrist first often means a long wait for an appointment that may turn out to be unnecessary.

Do I need both a prescriber and a therapist?

Often, yes, and that is a normal arrangement rather than a sign that something is severe. For many conditions, combining medication with psychotherapy outperforms either alone. In practice this usually means a psychiatrist or PMHNP handling medication every one to three months and a therapist handling weekly or biweekly sessions.
