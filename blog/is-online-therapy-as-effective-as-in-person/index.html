Is Online Therapy as Effective as In-Person Therapy?

The short answer: for therapy delivered over live video, yes, as far as randomized trials can tell. A 2023 meta-analysis of 20 trials found no meaningful difference between telemedicine and in-person treatment in outcomes, patient satisfaction, therapist relationship, or dropout. The important caveats are about scope. The strong evidence covers live video with a licensed clinician for conditions like depression, PTSD, and anxiety. It does not cover text-only services, and it does not cover bipolar disorder, psychosis, or active suicidality, because head-to-head studies there do not exist.

What does the research actually show?

The most direct evidence comes from randomized trials that assigned patients to the same treatment delivered either by video or in an office. That design isolates the delivery format, which is the question people are actually asking.

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in JMIR Mental Health pooled 20 such trials in PTSD, depressive disorders, and anxiety disorders. On treatment efficacy, the difference between telemedicine and in-person care was a standardized mean difference of -0.01 (95% CI -0.12 to 0.09) across 17 trials and 1,814 patients. That is as close to zero as these analyses get. Patient satisfaction and dropout rates were also comparable, and the authors rated the certainty of the efficacy evidence as moderate.

A separate 2022 meta-analysis looked at 12 randomized trials with 931 patients in less common territory: eating disorders, addiction, childhood mental health problems, and psychological support for chronic illness. It found no evidence of a difference between telehealth and face-to-face delivery there either, though the smaller trial base means less certainty.

None of this says online therapy is better. It says the delivery channel does not appear to be what determines whether therapy works. The treatment itself, and whether you stick with it, matters far more. Our guide to types of therapy compared covers how the actual treatments differ.

Is video therapy effective for PTSD specifically?

PTSD is worth singling out because it is the best-studied condition in this literature, mostly thanks to the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, which has run telehealth trials for years to reach rural veterans.

A VA evidence review of 50 studies, 24 of them on PTSD, concluded that PTSD symptom severity appears similar after in-home video and clinic-based in-person psychotherapy. Only 1 of 7 direct-comparison studies found a significant difference between formats. In one frequently cited randomized trial, prolonged exposure therapy delivered to 150 veterans by home-based telehealth was non-inferior to in-person delivery for reducing PTSD symptoms, both immediately after treatment and six months later.

That matters beyond veterans. Prolonged exposure is a demanding, structured treatment. If it survives the move to a laptop screen, the case that video is a weaker medium gets hard to sustain. For condition-specific treatment guides, see our sibling site ptsd.md.

Does video weaken the relationship with your therapist?

This is the most common intuition against online therapy: that something human is lost through a screen, and the relationship is where therapy's power lives.

The relationship question has been measured. Researchers call it the working alliance, and it predicts outcomes across nearly every form of therapy. In the 2023 meta-analysis, working alliance scores were comparable between video and in-person treatment across six trials, though the studies varied enough that this finding is less settled than the efficacy result. Dropout rates tell a similar story: across all 20 trials, patients were no more likely to quit video treatment than office treatment. People who feel disconnected from a therapist tend to leave, and that was not happening at higher rates online.

Individual fit still varies. Some people open up more easily from their own couch. Others find the screen distancing. Both reactions are normal, and neither shows up as a systematic effect in the trials.

When is in-person therapy the better choice?

The honest answer comes from where the evidence stops. The VA review found no telehealth-versus-in-person studies at all for bipolar disorder, serious mental illness such as schizophrenia, or patients in suicidal crisis. Equivalence has not been shown there because it has not been tested there, and higher-acuity situations generally call for care with more direct oversight.

Practical factors also point some people to an office:

  • No private space. Therapy from a car or a shared apartment is a real constraint, and privacy at home is a precondition the trials took for granted.
  • Crisis risk. If safety planning is an active part of your care, a clinician who can respond in person has options a video call does not.
  • Clinical observation. Some evaluations benefit from seeing the whole person, not a head and shoulders.
  • Technology friction. An unstable connection that drops mid-session is not a neutral inconvenience in an exposure exercise.

Safety, at least, does not appear to be a general problem: the VA review found serious harms associated with telehealth delivery to be rare in the studies that tracked them, while noting that few did.

Is texting a therapist the same thing?

No, and this is where the marketing outruns the evidence. The equivalence findings above come from live video sessions with a licensed clinician: a scheduled, real-time conversation that mirrors an office visit in everything but geography. Asynchronous text messaging, where you and a therapist trade messages over hours or days, was not what those head-to-head trials tested, and it has a much thinner evidence base.

If you are choosing an online platform, the research-backed format is live video with the same therapist each week. Whether the platform can also prescribe medication, and what it actually costs against alternatives like sliding-scale clinics, is covered in our guide to therapy costs without insurance.

How common is online therapy now?

Before 2020, telehealth was under 1% of outpatient mental health and substance use visits in the U.S. During the March to August 2020 period it hit 40% of those visits, and a year later it was still 36%, roughly five times the telehealth share seen in other kinds of outpatient care (KFF and Epic Research).

Mental health care went remote harder than any other specialty and largely stayed there. That is partly because talk therapy needs no physical exam, and partly because patients kept choosing it: shorter commutes, easier scheduling, and access to clinicians beyond driving distance, which matters most in the many U.S. counties with few or no local therapists.

The bottom line

If live video is what you can get, or what you prefer, the trial evidence says you are not settling for a weaker version of therapy. Outcomes, satisfaction, the therapist relationship, and completion rates all look about the same as office-based care for depression, PTSD, and anxiety. Choose in-person when your situation sits outside that evidence: higher-acuity conditions, active safety concerns, or simply no private place to talk.

The variable that dwarfs delivery format is whether you start at all. If you are weighing that question, our guide to when to see a therapist is the place to begin.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does online therapy work as well as in-person therapy?

Can you build a real relationship with a therapist over video?

The trial evidence says yes. In the 2023 JMIR meta-analysis, measured working alliance, the research term for the quality of the patient-therapist relationship, was comparable between video and in-person treatment across six trials, and patient satisfaction did not differ either. Dropout rates were also about the same, which is an indirect sign that people were not quietly disengaging.

The evidence gaps mark the boundaries. In-person care also makes sense when you lack a private space at home, when a clinician needs to observe physical signs, or in a crisis.
