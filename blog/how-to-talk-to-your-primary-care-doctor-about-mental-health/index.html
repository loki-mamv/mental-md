How Do I Talk to My Primary Care Doctor About My Mental Health?

The short answer: Say it plainly, and say it early in the visit: "I want to talk about my mental health today." Then describe your symptoms: when they started, how severe they are, and how often they occur. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) calls a primary care provider a good starting point. Write your concerns down before the visit, because appointments are short.

Is primary care the right place to start?

Yes, for most people. NIMH guidance on talking with a health care provider says a primary care provider can be a good starting point. It notes that mental health is linked to other conditions, such as heart disease and diabetes.

NIMH also says many primary care providers ask about anxiety, depression, or suicidal thoughts, and they can refer you to a mental health professional. If yours does not ask, bring it up yourself. The page is direct on this point: "Even if your PCP doesn't ask you first, take this opportunity to talk to your PCP."

You are not an unusual patient. NIMH statistics from the 2022 National Survey on Drug Use and Health estimate that 59.3 million US adults had a mental illness. That is 23.1% of adults. Only 50.6% of them received mental health treatment in the prior year.

How do you prepare before the visit?

Preparation matters because the visit is short. NIMH and the National Library of Medicine's MedlinePlus page on talking with your doctor recommend the same steps:

  • Write down your questions and concerns. Put the most important one first.
  • Write down your symptoms. Note when they started and what makes them better or worse.
  • List everything you take. Include prescription medicines, over-the-counter drugs, herbs, vitamins, and supplements.
  • Review your family history. NIMH notes that some mental illnesses run in families.
  • Note major stressors and recent life changes. A job loss, a death, or a new baby is useful context.

When you book, tell the scheduler the reason for the visit. A line such as "I want to talk about my mood" lets the office plan for that conversation. If a second problem also needs attention, ask if you need a separate appointment.

You can also bring a friend or relative. NIMH says a companion can offer support, take notes, and help you remember what the provider said. Some people meet the provider alone first, then ask the companion to join for the treatment discussion.

What do you actually say?

Start with one clear sentence. Do not wait until the doctor has a hand on the door. These openers work:

  • "The main reason I am here is my mental health."
  • "I have felt down most days for about two months, and it affects my work."
  • "I worry so much that I cannot sleep. I want to know what my options are."
  • "This is hard for me to say, so I wrote it down. Can I read it to you?"

Then give specifics. NIMH advises you to describe when your symptoms started, how severe they are, and how often they occur. Its list of symptoms to mention includes:

  • A persistent sad, anxious, or "empty" mood
  • Hopelessness or irritability
  • Changes in sleep, appetite, or energy
  • Aches or pains with no clear cause
  • Thoughts of death or suicide

Be honest, including about alcohol, drugs, and thoughts of self-harm. MedlinePlus puts it simply: do not say what you think the provider wants to hear. If you are not sure what counts as a reason to ask for help, our guide on when to seek therapy lists common signs.

What will the doctor ask or do?

Expect questions, and often a short questionnaire. The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommends depression screening for all adults, including pregnant and postpartum persons and adults 65 and older. It also recommends anxiety screening for adults 64 and younger. For adults 65 and older, it found the evidence on anxiety screening insufficient. Both statements are dated June 2023.

The forms have names such as PHQ-9 for depression and GAD-7 for anxiety. The USPSTF reports that a PHQ-9 score of 10 or more has a sensitivity of 0.85 and a specificity of 0.85 for depression. A score is not a diagnosis. The USPSTF states that screening tools alone cannot diagnose an anxiety disorder, and a positive result needs a confirmatory assessment. Our guide to what a mental health screening is explains the forms and the scores.

After that, your doctor will talk through next steps. Common ones are a referral to a mental health professional, a treatment that starts in primary care, or a follow-up visit. Many primary care doctors prescribe medicines for depression and anxiety. See our guide on getting mental health medication without a psychiatrist.

Ask questions before you leave. NIMH says you can ask for more information about a diagnosis or a treatment, and you can say so if an option makes you uncomfortable. Useful questions include:

  • "What do you think is going on?"
  • "What are my treatment options, and what do you recommend first?"
  • "If you refer me, how long is the wait, and what do I do until then?"
  • "When do you want to see me again?"

Is what you tell your doctor private?

In general, yes. NIMH states that conversations with your provider are private and are not shared without your permission. Ask your doctor to explain the limits if this worry holds you back.

What you say does become part of your medical record. Federal privacy rules give extra protection to one narrow category called psychotherapy notes. Under 45 CFR 164.501, these are notes that a mental health professional keeps apart from the rest of the record. They document or analyze a counseling session. The definition excludes medication prescription and monitoring, test results, and summaries of diagnosis, symptoms, and treatment plan.

For those separate notes, 45 CFR 164.508 requires your written authorization for most uses and disclosures. The practical point: a diagnosis or a prescription in your primary care chart is ordinary health information. Other clinicians who treat you can usually see it, which helps them avoid drug interactions. MedlinePlus suggests you learn how to access your own medical records, so you can read what the chart says.

What if the visit does not go well?

Sometimes the doctor seems rushed, or the plan does not feel right. You have options, and NIMH names them. You can ask about other treatments, and you can get a second opinion. NIMH also says there is no one-size-fits-all treatment. You may need to see several providers or try a combination of treatments before you find what works.

Try these steps:

  1. Say it again, more directly: "This affects my daily life, and I need a plan before I leave today."
  2. Ask for a referral to a mental health professional.
  3. Book a follow-up visit only for this topic.
  4. See a different primary care provider if you still feel unheard.

MedlinePlus advises you to ask questions until you understand, and to ask for written instructions if you need them. If your main concern is low mood, our sibling site depression.md covers symptoms and treatments in more depth.

What if you cannot wait for an appointment?

Do not wait if you have thoughts of suicide or you feel unsafe. Call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. The 988 Lifeline says the service is free and the conversation is confidential. You can also start a chat on its website. A live counselor answers, asks if you are safe, and listens. If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room.

The bottom line

Your primary care doctor is a good place to start, and NIMH says to raise the topic even if the doctor does not ask. Write down your symptoms, your medicines, and your questions before you go. Open with one clear sentence, and be honest about how bad it is. Expect a short questionnaire and a discussion of next steps. If the first visit does not help, ask again or see another provider.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I book a doctor's appointment only to talk about mental health?

Yes. Tell the scheduler that you want to talk about your mood, anxiety, or stress. NIMH says a primary care provider can be a good starting point and can refer you to a mental health professional.

Will my primary care doctor think I am overreacting?

It is a routine topic in primary care. The USPSTF recommends depression screening for all adults and anxiety screening for adults 64 and younger. NIMH data show that 23.1% of US adults had a mental illness in 2022.

Can I bring someone with me to the appointment?

Yes. NIMH says a friend or relative can offer support, take notes, and help you remember what the provider said. You can meet the provider alone first and ask your companion to join for the treatment discussion.

Does a PHQ-9 or GAD-7 score mean I have a diagnosis?

No. These forms are screening tools. The USPSTF states that screening tools alone cannot diagnose an anxiety disorder, and a positive result needs a confirmatory assessment by a clinician.

What if I do not agree with the treatment my doctor suggests?

Say so during the visit. NIMH says you can ask about other options or get a second opinion. It also notes that you may need to try more than one provider or treatment before you find what works.
