What Happens During a Psychiatric Evaluation?

The short answer: A psychiatric evaluation is a long, structured conversation with a mental health clinician. You describe your symptoms, health, family, and daily life. The clinician observes how you think and speak, checks for physical causes, and then explains a diagnosis and a treatment plan. No blood test or scan can diagnose a mental disorder, so the interview does most of the work.

Who does a psychiatric evaluation, and how long does it take?

A psychiatric evaluation is the full diagnostic visit. It is different from the short questionnaire you may fill out at a checkup. Our guide to mental health screening covers those forms and their scores.

Psychiatrists, psychiatric nurse practitioners, and psychologists do most evaluations. Only some of them prescribe medicine. Our comparison of psychiatrists, psychologists, and therapists explains who does what.

For adults, the American Psychiatric Association publishes practice guidelines for the psychiatric evaluation of adults. The current edition appeared in the American Journal of Psychiatry in 2015.

Expect the first visit to be longer than a normal follow-up. The length varies by clinic, so ask when you book. For children, the timeline is longer. The American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry says a comprehensive evaluation usually takes a few hours over one or more office visits for the child and parents.

What questions will you be asked?

Most of the visit is an interview. The clinician wants to know what is wrong, how long it has lasted, and how it affects your life. MedlinePlus lists the usual topics:

  • Mood and emotions
  • Sleep and appetite
  • Energy and concentration
  • Thoughts, fears, and behavior patterns
  • Alcohol or drug use
  • Family history of mental health conditions

You can also expect questions about your medical history, past treatment, and thoughts of suicide or self-harm. These questions are routine. They do not mean the clinician thinks you are in danger.

A little preparation helps. The National Institute of Mental Health recommends three things before the visit:

  1. Write down your questions and concerns.
  2. List every medication you take, including over-the-counter drugs, herbal remedies, vitamins, and supplements.
  3. Review your family mental health history.

NIMH also advises you to be specific. Say when each symptom started, how severe it is, and how often it occurs. Mention major stressors and recent life changes too.

What is the mental status exam, and will you need lab tests?

The mental status exam is the observation part of the evaluation. Much of it happens while you talk. MedlinePlus describes what the clinician checks:

  • Appearance and attitude. Grooming, posture, eye contact, and how you relate to the clinician.
  • Orientation. Your name, where you live, and the day, time, and season.
  • Attention. You may count backward by 7s or spell a word forward and backward.
  • Memory. You may be shown 3 items and asked to recall them after 5 minutes.
  • Language. You may name everyday objects or read and write a sentence.
  • Judgment. You may be asked what you would do if you found a driver's license on the ground.

Not every person gets every task. Clinicians use the formal memory and attention tasks most when they have a concern about thinking or memory. An abnormal result alone does not diagnose the cause, MedlinePlus notes.

Lab tests have a narrow job. MedlinePlus states that no medical test can diagnose a mental health disorder. But blood tests can show if a physical condition, such as thyroid disease or an electrolyte imbalance, causes the symptoms. If the clinician suspects a problem with your brain or nerves, you may have a neurological exam or brain imaging.

Will the clinician talk to your family or other doctors?

Sometimes, and usually with your permission. Clinicians call this collateral information. A partner or parent can describe changes that you did not notice.

NIMH suggests that you consider bringing a friend or relative. Some people keep that person in the room for the whole visit. Others meet the clinician alone first, and the companion joins for the treatment discussion.

For children, input from others is standard. AACAP says the evaluation includes interviews of the child and the parents. With the parents' permission, the psychiatrist may also contact the family physician, school staff, or other relatives.

What do you leave with?

You should leave with an explanation and a plan. AACAP calls the explanation a formulation. It describes the problem in terms the family can understand, and it combines biological, psychological, and social factors. When the clinician finds a treatable problem, they give recommendations and build a specific treatment plan.

The plan can include therapy, medication, more testing, or a referral. Sometimes the diagnosis is provisional, and the clinician confirms it over later visits. Ask these questions before you leave:

  • What is the diagnosis, and how sure are you?
  • What are the treatment options?
  • When is the next visit, and who do I call before then?

If the evaluation points to depression, depression.md covers that condition in more depth.

Is what you say confidential?

Yes, with a few limits set by federal privacy rules (HIPAA) and state law.

  • Your record is yours to see. Under 45 CFR 164.524, you have a right to inspect and get a copy of your health record. The rule excepts psychotherapy notes.
  • Psychotherapy notes get extra protection. These are a clinician's separate notes on the contents of counseling sessions. A provider needs your authorization for most uses of them under 45 CFR 164.508. Your diagnosis, treatment plan, medications, and test results are not psychotherapy notes.
  • Family involvement depends on you. Under 45 CFR 164.510, a provider may share relevant information with a family member if you agree or do not object. If you cannot decide because of incapacity or an emergency, the provider may use professional judgment about your best interests.
  • Safety is the main exception. 45 CFR 164.512(j) permits disclosure to prevent or lessen a serious and imminent threat to a person's health or safety.

State laws add their own rules, especially for minors. Our guide on what happens if you tell a therapist you want to hurt yourself explains the safety exception in practice.

How is an emergency evaluation different from an outpatient one?

An outpatient evaluation asks what is wrong and what will help over time. An emergency evaluation asks a narrower question first: are you safe right now?

In an emergency department, the visit starts with a medical check. Federal rules at 42 CFR 489.24 require a hospital with an emergency department to give a medical screening examination to anyone who comes in, regardless of ability to pay. The rule's definition of an emergency medical condition includes psychiatric disturbances and symptoms of substance abuse. The hospital may not delay the examination to ask about insurance.

After that check, the emergency evaluation focuses on risk and on the safest next step. That step can be home with follow-up, a crisis program, or a hospital stay. A full diagnostic workup often happens later, in an outpatient clinic.

If you are in crisis, do not wait for an appointment. The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline offers free, confidential support 24 hours a day by call, text, or chat.

The bottom line

A psychiatric evaluation is mostly a careful conversation. The clinician asks about your symptoms, history, and daily life, and observes your thinking and mood. Lab tests only rule out physical causes. You should leave with a working diagnosis, a treatment plan, and a next appointment. Bring a medication list, your family history, and your questions. Your record is protected by federal privacy rules, with exceptions for safety.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a psychiatric evaluation be done by telehealth?

Often, yes. MedlinePlus notes that mental status testing can be done in an office, a home, a hospital, or a telehealth visit. Blood tests or a physical exam still need an in-person visit. Ask the clinic which format it offers for a first evaluation.

Do I have to answer every question in a psychiatric evaluation?

No. You can say that you are not ready to discuss a topic. But the diagnosis depends on what you share, and NIMH advises open and honest communication. Tell the clinician if a question is hard, and return to it later.

Will I get a diagnosis at the first visit?

Many people do, but not everyone. Some diagnoses need lab results, records from other doctors, or more time to observe symptoms. The clinician may give a provisional diagnosis and confirm it at later visits.

Can I see what the psychiatrist wrote about me?

Yes, for most of your record. Federal privacy rules give you a right to inspect and get a copy of your health record. Psychotherapy notes, which are a clinician's separate notes on counseling sessions, are excepted from that right.

What should I bring to a psychiatric evaluation?

Bring a list of your questions, a list of all medications and supplements, and your family mental health history. NIMH recommends all three. You can also bring a trusted friend or relative to help you remember what was said.
