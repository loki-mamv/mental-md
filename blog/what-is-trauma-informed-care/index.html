What Is Trauma-Informed Care?

The short answer: Trauma-informed care is a way of running a clinic or service, not a type of therapy. Staff assume that any patient may have lived through trauma, and they shape the visit to avoid causing more harm. The best-known framework comes from SAMHSA, which names six principles, starting with safety. Research on outcomes is promising, but the studies are weak, so firm conclusions are not yet possible.

What does trauma-informed care mean?

It means the whole organization takes trauma into account, from the front desk to the leadership. The most widely used definition comes from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA). Its 2014 concept paper describes four steps, often called the four R's. A trauma-informed program:

  • Realizes the widespread impact of trauma and understands potential paths for recovery.
  • Recognizes the signs and symptoms of trauma in clients, families, and staff.
  • Responds by building knowledge about trauma into policies, procedures, and practices.
  • Resists re-traumatization, which means it avoids practices that repeat or recall past harm.

The same paper defines trauma broadly. Trauma results from an event, a series of events, or a set of circumstances. The person experiences it as physically or emotionally harmful or life threatening. It has lasting adverse effects on the person's functioning and well-being.

The VA's National Center for PTSD describes the aim in similar terms. Policies, procedures, and environments should support safety, healing, empowerment, and recovery. They should also reduce practices that cause stress or remind people of earlier trauma.

Where does the framework come from?

Two lines of work feed into it. The first is research on how common early adversity is. The original Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Study, published in 1998, surveyed 9,508 adults at a large health plan. More than half reported at least one category of childhood abuse or household dysfunction. The study found a graded relationship: more categories of exposure went with more health risks in adult life.

Newer national data point the same way. A CDC analysis of survey data from 2011 to 2020 found that 63.9% of US adults reported at least one adverse childhood experience. In the same analysis, 17.3% reported four or more.

The second line is SAMHSA's own work. Its Trauma and Justice Strategic Initiative published the concept paper in July 2014. The paper set out the four R's, six key principles, and ten implementation domains. In 2023, SAMHSA published a practical guide for implementing a trauma-informed approach. The guide keeps the same four assumptions and six principles. It adds steps for assessing readiness, putting changes in place, and evaluating them.

What are the six principles of trauma-informed care?

SAMHSA lists the same six principles in the 2014 paper and the 2023 guide:

  1. Safety. Staff and the people they serve feel physically and psychologically safe.
  2. Trustworthiness and transparency. The organization makes decisions openly, to build and keep trust.
  3. Peer support. People with lived experience of trauma help others recover. Our guide to peer support in mental health explains how that works.
  4. Collaboration and mutuality. Staff and clients share power and decisions. The paper quotes one expert: "one does not have to be a therapist to be therapeutic."
  5. Empowerment, voice, and choice. Clients take part in shared decision-making and goal setting.
  6. Cultural, historical, and gender issues. The organization moves past stereotypes and bias, and it recognizes historical trauma.

SAMHSA states that the approach reflects these principles, not "a prescribed set of practices or procedures."

How is it different from trauma therapy?

Trauma-informed care is the setting. Trauma-specific treatment is a clinical service inside that setting. The 2014 paper says a trauma-informed approach is "distinct from trauma-specific services." The 2023 guide defines trauma-specific services as clinical interventions that address trauma-related symptoms and PTSD directly.

The guide gives examples of evidence-supported trauma-specific treatments for adults:

  • Cognitive Processing Therapy
  • Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR)
  • Prolonged Exposure Therapy
  • Narrative Exposure Therapy
  • Seeking Safety

A dentist, a primary care office, or a school can be trauma-informed without offering any of these. A therapy practice can offer them and still fall short on the six principles. SAMHSA's guide says it is "not enough" to provide trauma-specific interventions. The culture, policies, and procedures must change too.

If you have symptoms of post-traumatic stress, you may need both. Our sibling site ptsd.md covers PTSD and its treatments. For a wider view, see our comparison of the main types of therapy.

What does it look like in a clinic visit?

You may not notice it as a separate thing. Much of it sits in how the building and the staff work. The 2023 SAMHSA guide gives examples of a physically safe setting:

  • Low noise levels
  • Signs that are warm, welcoming, and positive
  • Seating with adequate space between people and a clear view of who enters the room
  • Private areas where a person can calm down after a stressful moment

It also describes a psychologically safe setting. Clinical and non-clinical staff learn to greet people in a welcoming and respectful manner. Schedules are predictable, and staff give adequate notice of changes. Staff respect physical boundaries and offer options, such as leaving the office door open.

The guide says trauma screening should be universal and brief. Screening usually uses yes or no questions. A fuller assessment comes later, with a trained clinician, and should be rooted in trust and safety.

The approach covers what a service should stop doing. SAMHSA says organizations should not use practices that re-traumatize people. It names restraints, seclusion, and invasive procedures as examples.

Does trauma-informed care improve outcomes?

The evidence is limited. Early results lean positive, but most studies cannot show cause and effect.

A 2023 systematic review looked at trauma-informed programs with an organizational component. The search found 880 articles, and 15 met the criteria. The designs had no randomization or control group. The authors reported a "positive trend," including better functioning for the people served. They also wrote that low study quality made it "difficult to draw conclusions with certainty."

A 2024 meta-analysis pooled 13 randomized trials with 850 women who had experienced intimate partner violence. Depression and anxiety improved more with trauma-informed care than with usual care. PTSD symptoms did not differ between the groups at 3 or 6 months. The authors called the clinical efficacy "inconclusive."

A 2024 review of reviews covered 16 reviews in health care settings. It supported SAMHSA's ten implementation domains as a guide for health systems. It also noted that a lack of systems-level data has been a major barrier, and it called for more evaluation.

How can you ask for trauma-informed care?

You do not need to use the term, and you do not need to share your history. You can ask for the specific things that help you feel safe. These requests fit the SAMHSA principles of safety, transparency, and choice:

  • "Please tell me what you are going to do before you do it."
  • "I would like to stop or take a break if I ask."
  • "Can the door stay open?" or "Can someone come in with me?"
  • "I do not want to go into details today. Can we go at my pace?"
  • "What are my options, and what happens if I say no?"

When you choose a new provider, ask the office how its staff are trained in trauma-informed care. Ask what changes when a patient discloses trauma. Because SAMHSA sets principles and not a fixed checklist, the label alone tells you little.

It can help to plan what you want to say. Our guide on what to say in a first therapy session has wording you can adapt. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

The bottom line

Trauma-informed care is a framework for how services treat people. It is not a treatment for trauma. SAMHSA's version rests on four R's and six principles, led by safety, trust, and choice. Reviews show a positive trend but rate the evidence as low quality. You can ask for its practical parts at any visit: clear explanations, real choices, and the right to pause.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is trauma-informed care a type of therapy?

No. It is a framework for how an organization and its staff work with everyone. Therapies that treat trauma symptoms directly, such as Cognitive Processing Therapy or Prolonged Exposure, are called trauma-specific services. SAMHSA treats the two as distinct.

Do I have to talk about my trauma to get trauma-informed care?

No. The approach assumes that any patient may have a trauma history, so staff apply it to everyone. You can ask for clear explanations, choices, and breaks without sharing details. A fuller trauma assessment happens only with a trained clinician and should be built on trust.

What are the four R's of trauma-informed care?

They come from SAMHSA's 2014 concept paper. A trauma-informed program realizes the widespread impact of trauma, recognizes its signs and symptoms, responds by building that knowledge into policies and practices, and resists re-traumatization.

Is trauma-informed care only for people with PTSD?

No. The framework is meant for all people who use a service, with or without a diagnosis. A CDC analysis found that 63.9% of US adults reported at least one adverse childhood experience, so many patients have some history of adversity.

Is there strong evidence that trauma-informed care works?

Not yet. A 2023 systematic review of 15 studies found a positive trend but rated the studies as low quality, with no randomization or control groups. A 2024 meta-analysis of 13 trials found better depression and anxiety scores but no difference in PTSD symptoms at 3 or 6 months.
